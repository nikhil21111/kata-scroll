i.

Writing is thinking

The session opens with a confession and a show of hands: how many of you are bad writers? A few honest arms. How many want to improve at writing? Everybody. And then the claim the whole hour orbits: writing was always a type of thinking — active thinking, the visible part of an invisible act. Most people's writing is unclear, Varun says, because their thinking is unclear; the page is just the X-ray.

He anticipates the AI-era objection before anyone can raise it. Yes — you can upload ten documents of his old writing into a model and ask it to write like him, and it will nail the style: the rhythms, the vocabulary, the swagger. But it won't think like him. It won't end sentences the way he ends them, because it isn't extrapolating situations the way he does. It is mimicking his style, not his thinking — and that gap is exactly where the value lives. Style is copyable now. Thought is not.

And the warning underneath the comfort: it is remarkably easy to convince yourself that you've started to think clearly. The only reliable test of clear thought, the kata says, is contact with reality — when you've executed it and it has led to results. Writing is how thinking first leaves your head; execution is how it gets graded.

ii.

The literal genie

Then the famous bit. Varun pulls Rohit — who runs Yaas, the services company — up to the front, and makes him a promise with teeth: "I'm a genie. You get three wishes. But remember — I'm a KD genie. I have my own ulterior motives." What kind of genie? The worst kind: "I take things too literally. I'm Reddit."

The wishes come in, and watch what the literal genie does with them. A wish for infinite content ideas? The genie will slam his brain and synapses with infinite content ideas until he can't stand still. All the secrets of the universe? Every single one, jammed in simultaneously — hallucination, madness. Infinite money? The genie doesn't open a bank account — it dumps money on him until he drowns. Granting the wish and destroying the wisher, with the letter of the request satisfied the entire time.

The sophisticated players in the room try to outsmart the bit — "I want to own all the top companies in the world" — and the genie is ready: the instant some unknown owner appears on every registry overnight, the public markets panic and the stocks crash, the lawsuits from every legacy shareholder arrive, and you have inherited the full baggage of every company on earth. The wish was granted. That was the problem.

The only way to work with such a genie, the kata concludes, is precision of a nearly absurd order — "exactly $500,000, in exactly this bank account, delivered through non-fraudulent means, for work and services from a big company nobody would check." Every gap you leave, the genie fills. And now the pivot that turns a party trick into an operating manual: most AI and human employees are exactly like this genie — not maliciously, not to the extreme, but "to the extent of their context."

"I take things too literally. I'm Reddit."— the KD genie, before he grants your wish
iii.

Somebody else's things

Before the taxonomy, the ice cream. Tell an AI "generate a picture of an ice cream" — what color comes out? The room guesses: white, chocolate brown, raspberry purple. The machine picks at random, because you never said. Now the sting: if you asked your best friend which flavor to get you, they'd be right a hundred percent of the time. A co-worker you've shared an office with for a year? The room bets "chocolate" for one of their own — and he says no. Wrong. On ice cream. Now imagine this gap compounding across real work.

Hence the taxonomy of genies. The KD genie fills your gaps with negative context — your wish granted as a curse. A good genie fills them with positive context — routing your money through Singapore and Switzerland, handling the taxes, making the wish land softly. But the real world, the kata insists, is neither: "it's not positive or negative things. It's somebody else's things." Most AI and human employees can do whatever you ask — and if you don't provide enough context, they will do what is right for them and wrong for you, filling the gaps from what they know and understand.

Which quietly explains a pillar of the hiring market: companies hire for experience and taste so the employee can fill in the poor context that management gives. The worse you are at managing people, the more you must pay for experienced talent — you are outsourcing your clarity. Aeos started with mostly inexperienced people precisely because Varun was willing to sit on every video and explain the context himself; with editors whose taste already matched his — "everything you do, I agree with, looks good" — no filling was needed. You can be superb at the skill, but if your taste doesn't match the company's, you will always do your own thing.

iv.

The 30% tax

Now the number. In the communication between you and your teams, only 30% of what you mean is in your employees' heads. The rest — the why, the edge cases, the taste — dies in transit. What closes the gap is not talent but time: three years with his leadership, Varun says, and they know everything — his health issues, the car he likes, where he wants to buy a house, the foods he won't touch. So much shared context that either side can make decisions on the other's behalf — Rohit gone two weeks, Varun decides for him and is right; that interchangeability is what "years of experience in a business" actually compounds into.

And the way across the gap, for everyone you haven't spent three years with, is the humblest instrument in the kata: examples. "Pack each output file as a .wav in a folder called fixed files, each labeled by recording date and take number — 01_05_23_7.wav" — and suddenly everyone knows the folder, the naming, the contents, before a single file moves. Enough examples, and even the machine understands. Prompting gets its definition here: another word for writing — the act of providing context. Context is clarity: what is in your head.

v.

The missing spec

The kata's founding parable comes from Andy Grove, and it is two pancakes wide. Grove asks a new line cook to make pancakes and forgets to say "golden brown on both sides." The rookie flips once; one side is fully cooked, the other is undercooked goo; the customer sends it back; the trash can fills. The lesson, in the kata's exact words: the bottleneck is almost always a missing spec — a missing explanation or instruction, not missing skill. Missing skill is a YouTube tutorial away in any field on earth.

The prescription: write precise checklists; run one-on-ones — so the line cook's mental model matches the boss's. Left alone, veterans do fix the problem, but only because "the veterans invented their own checklist in their heads" — years of burnt pancakes as tuition, a far slower school than a single sheet. Some companies go further and hand new joiners a document titled what it's like to work with me — the kata calls this amazing: pages of context delivered on day one, instead of leaked over three years.

This reframes a sacred cow of the talent market: senior engineers aren't really three-times-faster coders. Think of them as context repair technicians. They know the codebase, they've solved this authentication problem before, they can decode what the company actually needs — and you overpay them, the kata says plainly, because management's briefs are underbaked. The premium isn't for typing speed; it's for repairing the gap your clarity left.

And the origin story ties the whole book together: Aeos stayed bootstrapped because of writing. Enough experiments were run before the company properly started that the founders knew exactly what they wanted — no need to hire a person for every role a funded competitor would fill with thirty salaries. The old team had cut three to four hundred scripts together: tonality, vocabulary, angles — all shared to the point where they create content on his behalf daily. "In the beginning I gave express instructions. Now it's a fully automatic process." Writing, the kata says, is how you abstract three years of meshing into day one.

"The bottleneck is almost always a missing spec — not missing skill."— the Andy Grove pancake, Kata 3
vi.

The pancake business

Now the kata's centerpiece experiment, run live on stage: start an error-free pancake business in Bangalore — the way Aeos was started, with lots of research and writing, and this time with AI. The prompt to the machine is a masterclass in itself: "I want to start a pancake business in Hebbal, Bangalore. I have ₹50K per month to spend, and I can spend a few months up front for equipment. Deep-research and tell me what I need to spend on, who to hire, and how to get customers. Make me a number-dense business plan for one year." Budget stated. Location stated. The one instruction that does the heavy lifting: "think about everything I've not thought about."

And then the moment Varun calls one of the best tools ever invented. Before answering, the deep-research genie does something employees almost never do: it asks you to fill in context. Do you plan to operate from a physical location? What kind of pancakes? Existing equipment, or everything from scratch? Operating hours and days? Cooking yourself or hiring a chef? Marketing channels — Instagram, delivery apps, local events? — "I wish," Varun says, "every time you wrote an instruction, an AI popped up and said 'good job man, I have five questions' — and added them to the bottom of your document." The same genie, now wearing glasses and a tie. (His confession: on a recent video proposal, the client's follow-up questions were completely predictable — "humans are not that special." He should have run the questions first.)

Then the second half of the method, the part most people skip: the AI test. The machine returns costs — staff salary ₹15,000 for a head cook, say — and you do not trust it. You pick up the phone. Walk into a restaurant: "Sir, I'm doing a research project — what's your salary?" Write the real number down. Call the family member who knows electricity bills. Check the flour prices at the market and multiply. Within about a day you have every edge case covered with specific real numbers — because AI supplies the edge cases (the checklist of everything you didn't think of) while humans supply the true numbers; the model's figures came from a five-year-old blog post. Then you feed the corrections back — "Sir, you are wrong; here's my updated list, here's my notebook screenshot" — and what comes out the other end is total cost, profit, and break-even, accurate enough that Varun runs the same loop at company scale.

And then — the step no AI can do: you pick up the phone and sell, and hire the people. The meshed world, in the kata's own words: AI does the skill stuff, and "all the people saying look at me, I'm so skilled — they're going to be useless." The people who pair the skill with the soft half — selling, hiring, running around — do fantastically well. Even the incentives come from the genie: "Boss, design incentives for the staff with a clear incentive for profitability, nobody wasting, good sales. Make it simple." Out comes a plan any restaurateur would recognize: hit ₹70,000 monthly sales and 5% becomes the bonus pool; keep food cost under 30% or forfeit it — a double incentive, hit the target AND don't cheat — split 60/40 between head cook and helper-cashier. The thousandth time smarter people have designed this, the kata shrugs; all that information is in the machine. Just give it your exact context.

vii.

Unreliable narrators

The kata now turns detective. Word reached Varun that work-life balance was "an issue" at Yaas — and rather than trust the story, he random-sampled four or five teams. What he found: new teams running two editors on a single shot a day, while the flagship team cuts a shot in thirty minutes. Watching closer: 50–60% of the day going to finding B-roll — scrolling, not editing. Somewhere nearby, a narrative formed about hard work and late nights — built, the kata notes, by someone who saw the night team arrive. Nobody sees the inputs. The output — a sad colleague at 9pm — becomes an "unreliable narrator," because output without why is just weather.

The layers underneath: new editors are roughly one-fifth as fast as editors with a year in — so "one shot a day" is fair at year one and brutal at month zero; the expectation didn't change, the experience did. And left unexamined, the org quietly redesigns itself around the distortion — two editors per shot as permanent architecture — which is waste the moment those editors get good. Unreliable narration, the kata rules, corrupts reporting and incentives both. For the incentive-gone-wrong hall of fame, Bangalore real estate: buildings that won't rent a floor below a price — leave it empty for five years — because renting at ₹80 what was "worth" ₹100 drops the valuation of all twelve floors and the stock that rides on it. Incentives, fully wrong, perfectly rational.

The fix is boring and works: everyone reports what they've done — the business runs on autopilot without the leader Zumba-ing the chef daily. The gold standard, held up by name: Ranit Magnani's weekly hiring report, straight WhatsApp — total hired this week: eight (seven video editors, one freelance infographic designer); joining next week: twelve, the highest week yet; and honest notes: we didn't close any script-writer or presenter — quite bad, lots of bottlenecks to remove. A company of such reports steers itself. One caution for the dashboard-minded: intent doesn't show up in KPIs. Five videos a month proves output, not caring — the person could be brain-dead and still ship five.

viii.

Clarity is power

The closing movement assembles the ethics. First, write down your workloads and problem statements — tasks ranked by priority: this channel needs an editor, that one needs short-forms doubled, editor A needs references, editor B gets one month and a clear bar. Then the humane version of the hard conversation: "Within 60 days you need to be editing a shot of this quality — here's the reference, here's where you're lagging." Most people are never told where they're lacking — they just eventually get fired and think what the hell? Clarity is not harshness; it is the only version of firing that can be called prudent.

Second, the guardrail on that power: the sunshine test. If what you said to a colleague were printed on tomorrow's front page, would you be ashamed? "I fired this person for the right reason — I'll stand by it" passes. The sentence you'd be afraid to see in print doesn't get said. Assume everything is public — at a company doing close to a billion views a month, it functionally is.

Third, the arithmetic of leadership: weak managers are afraid to fire low performers, and in exchange lose high performers — nobody excellent will stay in the group project where they do 90% of the work. You are as bad as your weakest link, and coordination cost is a real bill. And for a sense of the volume at the top, Varun publishes his own receipts: ~20 emails a day, 80–90 work WhatsApps, one to two living Google Docs — and thrives is what you'll do on a tenth of it. Slack, WhatsApp, Docs: "that's where the electricity flows."

The destination, and the closing grace note: work with your AI closely enough, now, that it knows you — the day is close when it drafts the feedback email because it knows you and knows where this person is lacking. And — this is a long sport. No point burning out in three months; the fast companies were ten-year journeys wearing three-year costumes. Everyone wants it in their early twenties; it's okay if it takes time — the destination, once reached, is boring anyway. What Aeos actually is, at its best: the closest thing to being backstage at WWE in the peak years — everything public, everything commented on, a great place and a great responsibility. Enjoy the ride.

"Your clarity as a leader — or lack of it — can make or break careers."— Kata 3, the closing charge
ix.

Practice —
this is your kata

Clarity is a muscle; vagueness is a habit. This week's repetitions:

Take your last disappointing AI output and audit the wish: list every gap the genie filled with somebody else's things — each one was yours to specify.
Run one instruction through the pancake loop: precise prompt → let the machine ask its five questions → answer with real, phone-checked numbers.
Convert one recurring vague request into a spec with examples — the .wav-folder test: could a stranger produce your intent from the page alone?
Find your pancake: one undercooked expectation on your team — write the golden-brown-both-sides version, with a reference and a deadline.
Run one report the Ranit way: what was done, what's joining, what's blocked — three lines, no spin, every week.
x.

Sit with these

— What did my last "bad AI output" actually reveal about the wish I made — and whose things filled my gaps?

— Where is my 30% landing — and whose defaults am I paying senior salaries to repair?

— Which of my frustrations is an unreliable narrator: output I've read a story into, without ever seeing the inputs?

— And the sunshine test, honestly: which of this week's messages would I not want printed on tomorrow's front page?

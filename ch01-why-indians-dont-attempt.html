i.

The anti-attempt
nation

Open with the quote the whole session stands on, because Varun does — a line he attributes to Steve Jobs and repeats like a company prayer: "Somewhere between the janitor and the CEO, excuses stop mattering." The janitor can say the bathroom door was locked; fair excuse. The executive standing in front of the same locked door has options — find the key, ask for it, cut a duplicate, break the door down, call someone who will. Somewhere on the climb, the number of ways through any door grows so large that why it stayed closed stops being an answer anyone accepts.

Now the diagnosis the chapter is named for. India, Varun says flatly, is a very anti-attempt nation — not for lack of talent, but because we are so afraid of taking an attempt and being ridiculed that we don't even try. And the fear has a precise anatomy: after an attempt, you find out. You find out whether the story of yourself in your head is true or not. It is the anxiety of the medical test — everyone dreads the checkup, then walks out without the disease. 99% of the country never attempts hard things, because the act of trying, which almost always fails at first, feels like the diagnosis rather than the screening.

Against that fear, the session's two counter-prayers. First, from Mark Zuckerberg, after a live Ray-Ban demo in which several things visibly failed: "I'm happy we got three out of five things right." A trillion-dollar CEO, stock price on the line, the internet watching — and completely okay. Second, the house rule Varun repeats at open and close: failure cannot be demonized — failure is the norm — hang out with people who are okay with failing loudly. The room that makes failing shameful is the room where nobody swings.

ii.

The scouter

The instrument. In Dragon Ball, a scouter is the device that reads a fighter's power level the moment you point it at them — and Varun's claim is that the real world has one too. Not a number, but a reliable read of a person's actual value: can they do what they claim — can they, in fact, write the check? Can the angel actually invest, the client actually decide, the candidate actually deliver? You need this read for two mirror reasons, he says: as a hirer, to tell who has how much potential, and as the hired, to know what you must actually have before the raise, the appraisal, or the offer ever comes.

And the first calibration point of the whole instrument is uncomfortable: reading competence starts with reading your own. Some in the room undervalue themselves; others are overconfident — "somebody at another startup makes slightly more, I'm better than them, therefore I deserve more." The scouter exists to replace those feelings with a reading — and among competent people, the reading obeys one law stated without exceptions: the person who has done something is taken more seriously than the person who has not, however elegant the non-doer's take-down of the doer may be.

"The person who has done something is taken more seriously than the person who has not. No exceptions for eloquence."— the scouter's first law
iii.

Two Startup
Indias

Before the levels, the confession. Varun admits he spent years as one of the people in funded founders' Twitter replies — "bro, company profitable?" — looking at startups burning cash and concluding they didn't know what they were doing. What he couldn't see from outside: they were playing a different game. Some companies play for singles — one run, one run, one run. Others walk to the crease trying to hit a six every single ball, because the game they've chosen is all-or-nothing, monopoly-or-nothing — a game where raising enormous money to outlast every competitor is the correct move, not a failure to understand profit. (You will meet this arithmetic again in the finale, from the other side of the table — the investor who would rather the company die aiming at the moon than limp home at a clean 20%. Same mathematics, different seat.)

Hence the map he draws of his own industry: two Startup Indias. Startup India 2 — the tier-two VCs, the unknown companies, good people, no spotlight. Startup India 1 — the Lenskarts, Zomatos, Creds; the names that come to your head. He lived in 2, without an intro into 1, without a performed enough miracle — and so he looked at 1 with skepticism: they're doing it wrong. There was even a year he ran a profitable company with small numbers and quietly compared himself to a startup that had burned a hundred crores — and five years later, that company was doing very, very well. The scouter had misread the game.

And how did he get into Startup India 1? The lesson the section exists to teach: "opportunities are not given. You grind enough, and be visible enough, and perform enough miracles." He had always believed he deserved to be in Startup India 1 — just like the candidate he negotiated with recently, who said "I think I'm worth this much, because I deserve it," and got the most important reply in the chapter: "You believe in this, but none of your outcomes prove this. The minute you prove it, you'll make far more than this." The gap between I think I can do it and I have done it is, in Varun's estimate, a few years wide — the difference between vegetables on a table and an actual dish. Everyone can buy the vegetables.

iv.

The test

Now the mechanism — the exact sequence by which a power level rises from zero toward 9,000. Aeos has a name for it, and the kata builds it in front of you. First, you believe in something — genuinely, a belief you'd defend. Second, you take a big swing at it with personal risk attached. The risk is not decoration; a gaming publisher told Varun the cleanest version: "I can write this check, but I won't — I need six months." Why? "In our company, you're allowed to take any bet. The company trusts you. But if you're wrong about the bet — you lose your job." You give a person power, and you attach the price of missing. Third, the cards are bad and the skepticism arrives — people believe it won't work, ridicule is normal (Qualcomm's CDMA was laughed at; the valley's default is to try anyway). Fourth, somehow, you pull it off — and the minute you do, the reading changes, permanently, because you have displayed competence.

The kata is scrupulous about the near-misses, because they are where most careers quietly die. Predictive confidence — making a video or a thread about the future without swinging — earns a sliver of credit: he was right about a couple of things, maybe he'll be right about the third. Belief without attempt — the principled vegan who never bends — earns respect for standing for something, and is also the biggest trap in the chapter: you can believe garbage for a lifetime and never find out. And the assisted swing — the billionaire's kid who pulls off the project with parental millions — earns a discounted reading, because everyone can see the help; which is, the kata notes, exactly why children of the successful should choose harder and harder projects.

And the twist that makes the test survivable: even a failed swing earns competence if the attempt is clean. You went for six, the wickets fell — but you gave it everything, and the market beat you. What is not clean: the three-month fade — realize it isn't working, ask for a department switch, quietly quit, move on. The clean version, the Aeos way, is to put your hand up: "I thought I could do this. I don't think I can. Here are the reasons — here are the gaps in my own knowledge. I'm shutting it down cleanly." A clean failure usually earns you another shot. But at some point, you must deliver.

v.

The five power levels

The instrument's dial. Five levels — and two warnings before the cards. These are not levels of money: an NGO founder who has made little but made a hard social movement happen ranks above a lucky stock-picker worth more. And they are not birthrights — Varun has watched people he graded as NPCs leap levels within a year of exposure to risk. It is a free market, and the dial moves. The front of each card is the level as its residents live it; the back is how to spot it, and the exit.

Level 0
The NPC

No beliefs, no attempts, pure consumption. The disillusioned mode — the world didn't reward them early, so they retired from it without announcing retirement. Sitting somewhere, really scrolling.

turn the card ↻

how to spot

Contributes nothing to any game; consumes everyone else's. The hermit's silence is not peace — it's unread drafts of swings never taken.

the exit

One small belief, one small visible action, real name attached. The level moves before the belief deepens.

一
Level 1
The Commentator

Very strong opinions, zero real attempts — a courage issue, not an intelligence one. The meeting signature: "this needs to be done, that needs to be done" — then, asked to do it: silence. The kata's shorthand: hi Twitter and Reddit, no P&L.

turn the card ↻

how to spot

Passion everywhere stakes are absent. The elegant public take-down of a doer, written from the stands.

the exit

Convert one opinion into a position — publish a prediction, ship a small version of the thing you critique. Skin in the game is the toll.

↺ return to the front

二
Level 2
The Local Doer

Small attempts, your own initiative, got somewhere — freelancers, side-hustlers, the kid on Instagram shipping while commentators make fun of them. Low personal risk, some wins — and a real scouter reading for exactly that reason.

turn the card ↻

how to spot

Ask what they've made: the answer is a thing, not a plan. The portfolio is short but it exists.

the exit

Repeat in public, raise the stakes one notch at a time — the path to level three is volume of swings, not size of one.

↺ return to the front

三
Level 3
The Owner

The level Varun wants to rename from "founder" to "owner" — because you own whatever you're running: repeated high-risk attempts with skin in the game. The rule is hard: you cannot take full market salary AND equity. Some here do the honest math — cut their own salary to hold a share of the swing.

turn the card ↻

how to spot

Something exists that can fail and hurt them: a P&L, a payroll, a name on the door. Look in their past: hidden failures — the only way to know what you can do is by trying, and the first ten attempts mostly don't work.

the tell

Usually after the first attempt you can tell who has the owner mindset: those who try once, fail, and refuse the public humiliation of a second — never try again.

↺ return to the front

四
Level 4
The Kingmaker

Can move markets, industries, or cultures with a decision. Multiple successful events — and the strange immunity: so hedged by accumulated social capital that a failure or two barely dents the reading. Elon ships a dud; your opinion of him doesn't move. Even their failures get rescued by the ecosystem.

turn the card ↻

how to spot

The difference from level three: three is still sometimes dealt bad cards; four walks in holding the entire deck, deciding what to do with it.

the honest note

You don't chase this level — you get pulled into it, the way chapter fourteen's "thousand doors" gets pulled toward legacy. Keep swinging; the market promotes.

vi.

The penalty &
the repricing

The dial moves down as well as up, and the kata names the force: the scouter penalty — multiple similar failures without updating your model. The engineer who builds app after app, never thinking about what he builds, hoping the next one goes viral — the gambler going all-in every round, waiting for the one hand where everyone else holds bad cards. It doesn't work that way. The first failures cost you the sting; the repeated same failures cost you the reading.

Which is why the highest-level people share one habit the kata insists on: they cleanly, brutally explain why their last attempt worked or failed — without blaming external factors. Wrong market: my fault. Wrong people: my fault. Pandemic: still my fault, for not planning contingencies — the BlackRocks of the world plan for every one; what exactly is different about you? The person who left, poached talent, watched that company die — and still blames the funding environment — takes the penalty twice. The self-blamer updates the model; the blamer just files the excuse.

And the upside compounds the same way. One clear win permanently upgrades how serious people see you — the kata's exhibit: the Hotmail founder, one success twenty years ago, still carrying the respect of everyone who hears the story. You just need one. Humanity, Varun notes, simply loves these stories — the big swing, made. That's why the level goes up and people talk about you at that exact moment: you've displayed competence, and competence displayed is the only advertisement the competent trust. Access to more capital, more talent, more budgets follows the reading like weather follows pressure.

vii.

The competence
blindspot

Why does a nation of graders misgrade so badly? The kata's answers, in order. India is a populist country that loves a good story — and in our stories, risk and luck simply do not exist. We attribute another's success to luck ("some VC just gave him money" — not seeing the two-year relationship that the raise was capitalizing) and our own success to skill. We cloud our own insecurity and ego into the reading of others: two young kids doing well must have rich parents. The truth underneath: all success stories have luck involved — but luck only finds you after you attempt seriously.

Then the deeper bias — the national ideal of the competent person, straight off Reddit: quiet, shy, behind the scenes, introverted, suppressed, very technical, humble, conflict-avoidant. We love CTOs; we ascribe the quiet one as "the person who actually built everything" — which is almost never how companies run. Both co-founders matter; whole teams matter. The reality the kata has met in every founder with a genuinely good P&L: verbal and social; conflict-resolving, not conflict-avoiding — "you two fight it out, finish, this is the direction; we disagree but commit"; charming, because the world opens for charm and closes for pitches; and salespeople — money does not come from being behind the scenes. Ninety percent of the successful people he's seen with positive P&Ls are run by salespeople.

And the pairing that runs the best companies: product AND marketing — Jobs, Musk, and (the underrated case) Sam Altman, who is not the most technical person in his company, knows enough — about 90% — and hires the rest. Because product is mostly marketing: how humans behave when they see your ad, flow through your app, click, watch, buy. "Product starts from the day a person sees your ad." The technical implementation is real — but it is the hireable 10%, and the market prices the whole stack.

The blindspot has a mirror: you can dislike a person and they can still be competent — two separate problem statements. Reddit calls Elon incompetent; a politician called him "the dumbest billionaire," corrected by the audience to almost trillionaire. Dislike the man if you like; misread the reading and every bet you place on people goes wrong with it.
"All success stories have luck involved. But luck only finds you after you attempt seriously."— Kata 4, the luck law
viii.

Reading scouters
in others

The instrument earns its keep in four rooms. Hiring: prefer the track record of repeated attempts over any CV design or college — the questions almost nobody asks: what attempt have you taken, what is the output, what did you actually contribute — the exact spec? Not "last company paid this, therefore we pay this." Aeos interviews for whether you've worked at a profitable startup and how much of that was you. Choosing your boss: the chapter's hardest rule — your growth ceiling is the max cap of your boss's scouter level. Managers don't let reports out-level them; if the boss's level is low, no idea of yours ever rises. You're not joining a company, the kata says; you're joining a manager — and if the manager can't give you space to grow, ask for a team switch.

Choosing co-founders: avoid people who have never taken personal risk — "they just don't get it." The person holding equity without parental wealth probably took zero salary for a year and worked off savings; entitlement to salary-plus-percentage without understanding that arithmetic is a misread of the whole game. Choosing your circle: high levels cluster — the competent love talking to the competent and skip the small talk (Varun listens to people at 2× speed, YouTube-style, to reach the point). Anyone ahead of you is a chance to learn — ego is the enemy — and the secret of hiring, stated plainly: find low levels with potential, and unlock them over time.

Two cautions close the reading. Schadenfreude — the German word for feeling good when others lose, abundant in India — corrupts the scouter by turning it into a social process: scoring people by committee, envy, and missing context, when scouting is an individual relationship between you and that person. And the culture proof, from Varun's Dota years: the Garena exodus to Ranked Gaming Client, a community that never let anyone below a certain level in and never lowered the bar — which is, he says, exactly what Aeos is. High competence brings high competence. The bar holds.

"Your growth ceiling is the max cap of your boss's scouter level."— the boss rule, Kata 4
ix.

Raising your level

The kata closes its teaching with the actual playbook — five instructions, each with a story attached. Pick a game where outcomes are measurable. A marketing person at an OTT platform couldn't prove anything: every campaign had fifty people in it. Play games with a scoreboard — revenue, users, rank, watch time. Place a visible personal bet — money, reputation, years of your life; the test is not the test without the price. Ship in public, and let people see you struggle and iterate. When someone goes fully silent for three months, Varun knows they're about to quit; visible struggle, he has learned, is the thing that makes people come help you. Ignore ridicule; collect actionable feedback — the person who tells you the specific thing to improve is gold; the person who just laughs is weather. And remember that one clear win permanently upgrades you — it is hard for people to keep giving you shots, so when the swing comes, take it.

Underneath all five, the frame the kata wants you to keep: none of this has changed in 200,000 years. What's valued is bravery, confidence, trying, leadership, teamwork — the tools changed; the reading didn't. Nothing is handed to you. Default skepticism is fine. The onus is on you to prove it.

And for the days the scoreboard itself is fog — when you genuinely don't know what to do — the kata reaches for two borrowed lines. Brian Armstrong, CEO of Coinbase: "Action produces information." If you don't know what to do, just do something, and you'll find out whether it works. And Ilya's favorite answer to the unanswerable: "the answer will reveal itself" — not in planning, but in playing. Varun's own proof: nobody at Aeos predicted that Unleash the Avatar would go viral in China, sitting a few views below the GTA 6 trailer on Instagram there — there was no way to know without playing the card. You don't need perfect information before you go after the thing. You just need to do.

x.

Practice —
this is your kata

A reading of levels is entertainment until it is repeated against reality. This week's repetitions:

Read your own scouter honestly: what have I attempted, visibly, with real personal risk — and which level does that make me?
Pick a measurable game this quarter — revenue, users, rank, watch time — and place one visible bet on it, with something real attached.
Convert one opinion into a position — publish it under your real name where being wrong is possible.
Take your last failure and write the clean version: my fault, the gaps in my knowledge, the model I'm updating. No external factors.
Ship one thing in public, struggle visible — and if you're hiring, ask one candidate the question: what did you actually contribute, exact spec?
xi.

Sit with these

— What have I believed in strongly, for years, without ever once swinging at — and is it belief, or the trap?

— Where in my life am I the janitor with a locked door — and where am I the executive pretending the janitor's excuse still covers me?

— Whose scouter have I misread because I dislike them — or because the room was scoring by committee?

— And the test's honest question: one clean swing from now, what number does the room read?

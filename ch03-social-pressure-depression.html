i.

The secret kata

This session was never meant for the internet. Varun built it for the Lightning Core — the sixty or seventy people at the center of the company — and the reason it stayed internal is the chapter's first harsh truth: there are scientific truths that, said in public, will get you belted. It is remarkably easy to offend Indians, he notes, precisely because Indians reliably respond — and he only published this one at all because two people on his team, Saga and Shans, insisted: just do the first one, and see what happens. If I get belted, it's their fault.

The framing he protects above all: "I am not a doctor" — but everything in the deck is doctor- and licensed-therapist-validated, peer-reviewed, cited, links in the description. The lens is business, not medicine: what leadership becomes once you understand what is happening, chemically, inside the people in the room. Because the thesis of the whole secret series is that the only way to become very good at leadership is to control your own neurotransmitter and hormone profile — and manage that of others. Serotonin is episode one. And the session opens with its own show of hands — how many of you have had depression? Five or six hands in a room of builders.

ii.

What stress
actually is

First, a correction of the pop version. Everyone has heard low serotonin causes depression — and the kata is careful: the real science says that model is too simple, and often wrong. Serotonin plays a role; the meta-reviews tell a far more complicated story. The better frame — and the one this entire chapter runs on: serotonin is the social safety chemical. High, you feel socially safe. Low, you feel socially unsafe. Not happiness versus sadness — safety versus threat.

Now the research definition of stress, aimed like a weapon at the confusion the whole country shares. Indians have equated stress with hard work — it's making me work hard, that must be stress. No. Stress is something you cannot reliably overcome — or a social situation in which you cannot reliably obtain any status. In the lab, if you want to give an animal depression, you use exactly this: social defeat stress, the reliable model. Put the rat in a hopeless situation — and watch the line that divides illness from health: if the animal is even allowed to crawl out a little bit, it doesn't get depression. Hopelessness is the pathogen. And chronic exposure doesn't just darken the mood — it physically reshapes the serotonin circuits.

Which makes depression a biopsychosocial disease: genes set the susceptibility — how fast, how hard, how resilient — but the environment pulls the trigger. "If your environment sucks and is very against you, you will reliably get depression." The kata's gentle everyday proof: see good food after a while, and your mouth waters — environment moves chemistry, in everyone, constantly. Mood is not exempt.

"Stress is something you cannot reliably overcome — or a social situation where you cannot reliably get any status."— the kata's operating definition
iii.

The brain under
defeat

What chronic social defeat does to hardware. Two structures matter. The prefrontal cortex — impulse control, executive function, emotional regulation; the thing that keeps you from doing the stupid thing. The amygdala — the brain's threat detection center, fast and emotional. Under sustained social defeat, the PFC atrophies — physically smaller, volume lost, connectivity thinned — while the amygdala goes hyperactive. The brake wears down while the alarm gets louder. That is the machine the rest of the chapter is managing.

Now run the software. When the brain learns I'm losing in a status game, it starts scanning for danger, and ordinary life re-prices itself: ambiguous messages start feeling insulting — someone glances at your dirty shoes and you read a judgment that was never sent; feedback feels like humiliation; silence feels like rejection — the person not replying becomes evidence; somebody else winning hurts worse than it should. The clinical name for the extreme form: rejection sensitivity dysphoria — RSD — an extreme emotional sensitivity to perceived rejection or criticism, with serotonin-transporter down-regulation linked to anxiety-like behaviors that mirror it.

The kata's unflinching look at what this does to a nation online: people across the world are racist to Indians on Twitter and make ad money from it — because they know Indians will respond. Other countries don't take the bait; there's no engagement to farm. The American founder hears a no and shrugs more often; in India, anyone you reject will take offense — which is why Indian VCs deliver their no like a bomb-disposal team: sorry, we are wrong sometimes, don't feel bad, don't feel offended. They know their audience. And the counter-practice Varun installs at Aeos, said in every appraisal: "A no for this appraisal doesn't mean your value is less. It has nothing to do with you. It's a business decision — does it fit the budget? Rejection is never personal. Sometimes it is — but not here."

iv.

The rank game

Why the whole country runs hot. India — in Varun's opinion and many scholars' — is a high rank-pressure environment, and the audit is quick: competitive exams where one person wins and lakhs lose; limited seats for everything, from the exam to the concert; constant comparison — Sharma ji ka beta — now industrialized by feeds that show you only billionaires; and the school class rank, which — did it ever actually matter? No. A game played for stakes that never existed, installed into a nervous system that never forgot it. Everything in the society is a rank game, and rank games produce the chemistry of the last section at population scale.

One more cruel property, and the kata doesn't soften it: social defeat stress is infectious. Hang out with five people in bad mood, criticizing everything — you become them. The exhibit: Reddit — people mildly dissatisfied drift in, and everyone who has sat there a long time is feeling socially suppressed, so to feel safe there, you adopt the position that the rest of society is unsafe. "It's like going down the toilet for your brain — whatever the equivalent of that is." Environment is chemistry; you are the average of the rooms you sit in.

v.

The chemistry of safety

Now the human evidence, told honestly. Years ago — six, seven — physical health problems dragged Varun's mental state down with them, and he was prescribed antidepressants for a period. What he noticed surprised him: not happiness, not giggling. Less reactivity. Situations that should have irritated him simply didn't register; someone tried to offend him and it bounced off a pane of glass. The common complaint — "you become a zombie" — was, from the inside, a quieter, less-reactive mind. Clinically: SSRIs normalize the amygdala's hyperactivity — the alarm turns down, and with it threat reactivity and negative bias.

Which finally explains the sentence a college friend named Rial gave him long before any of this made sense: "It's not bad thoughts that lead to bad mood. It's bad mood that leads to bad thoughts." Bi-directional, he notes — but the arrow most people forget is the one running from chemistry up to thought. The intellect arrives after the mood, like a lawyer, and writes the brief the mood demands. You do not think your way into the pit; you are lowered into the pit, and your thinking is hired to redecorate it.

And the extreme case that proves the mechanism — MDMA. The minute people take it, they feel socially safe: vulnerable, unguarded, unoffended — not worried about judgment for a few hours (multiple systems at once, the kata notes — too simple to credit serotonin alone). Then, three days later: the Tuesday blues — temporary depression, everything offending them, easy to make them shout. The chemistry of safety, drained and overcorrected. Two states of the same brain, and both are for sale on every street corner of the internet.

"It's not bad thoughts that lead to bad mood. It's bad mood that leads to bad thoughts."— Rial, in college; the kata's spine
vi.

What the monkeys
taught

The studies you cannot ethically run on humans — which is why, the kata says, secret katas are secret. Vervet monkeys: the minute a male ascends to the alpha position, his blood serotonin rises by roughly fifty percent. Lose the position — back to baseline. Put him back up — up again. Even the reverse: a drug (cyproheptadine) that reduces blood serotonin de-thrones him. Chemistry follows rank, in real time, in both directions. (Varun's aside, aimed squarely at the internet's alpha: "Andrew Tate's definition of an alpha is completely wrong — I wish he read some books.")

And what the actual alpha looks like, per the research: less aggressive, more collaborative and assertive — the higher a monkey's serotonin function, the more likely it is to affiliate and collaborate. Meanwhile the low-serotonin profile is impulsive aggression, risk-taking, hyper-reactivity — not calculated risk, cliff-jumping risk. The subordinates who try to fight their way up without the chemistry to hold it become volatile; they might win a physical fight by luck, but they cannot build the alliances to rule — and here the kata translates to business in one line: people leave to build their own company and immediately pick a fight with everybody, missing the memo of why companies actually succeed. Sam Altman, by contrast, has made every company in the US collaborate with him in some way. The best out there are collaborating all the time; the rogues rule by fear and are eventually overthrown by a coalition of calmer, more cooperative males.

Two studies close the exhibit. Sapolsky's Type-A baboons: the ones that saw threats everywhere — unable to distinguish a major rival from a minor annoyance, lacking social outlets — had the worst psychological profiles regardless of rank; the true alphas, in contrast, simply ignored minor provocations — small things said about them bounced off; they saved the alarm for real threats. And juvenile rhesus monkeys given fluoxetine — an antidepressant — started grooming each other more: affiliative, collaborative, repeatable. Monkey studies don't map one-to-one onto humans, the kata warns; but the pattern rhymes everywhere it's tested.

vii.

The golden rules

From research to protocol — the operating rules for an environment that doesn't manufacture the disease. One: praise in public, correct in private — and private means small: two or three people, ideally one. Two: critique the work, not the identity. Varun is famous for looking at something and saying "this is trash" — and is very clear about the line: the work is garbage, you are never garbage. Three: lead with alignment before disagreeing. Not "that's stupid" — but "you're right, I agree with your line of thinking — here are the three things you're missing." The extra thirty seconds that keep the PFC online.

Four: make evaluation criteria defined. No secret evaluations, no "oh, you did well this week, here's a promotion" — a person must always be able to see, in writing, how the path to the promotion runs. Five: blameless postmortems. Never "we failed because of you" — ask what happened, what we learn, how we get better, with no person named. If a person genuinely is the problem: let them go — fire them — but there is no point blaming a person after the failure. Both things are true: no scapegoats, and no squatters. Six: protect status while changing behavior — correct the action without de-throning the human. Do this while scaling, the kata warns, or the company arrives at people fighting each other anyway.

viii.

The balancer's
toolkit

For the leader's own skull, the kata teaches its most personal technique — simulation. Varun vividly imagines the worst case for everything the company is doing: going bankrupt, everything getting automated, public humiliation — visualizes it fully. This is amygdala habituation: by voluntarily exposing the brain to the simulated threat in a controlled environment, the response is dampened. The monster becomes known. His example is legal threats: the first one ruins your week — imagined jail, imagined ruin; experienced entrepreneurs carry hundreds of cases and are simply habituated. They already know the worst case and are willing to live with it. The reframe underneath: the entrepreneur realizes they would survive the defeat — and the fear of defeat is often more paralyzing than the defeat itself. It mirrors the CBT move of de-catastrophizing — the one that talks a kid who missed the college cutoff back from the ledge: what actually happens now? I'll figure it out anyway.

Then the enemy inside: the inner critic — the internalized voice of social defeat, the Sharma-beta mechanism running in your own head. The research the kata cites is brutal: negative self-talk activates the same stress pathways as external verbal abuse — attacking yourself in your head is, chemically, as good as someone abusing you from outside. It keeps cortisol high and serotonin low. The technique that works: distanced self-talk — refer to yourself in the third person under stress. "Varun, you didn't do a very good job of this — here's what you'd do better." That small window of distance has been shown to reduce amygdala activation — it lets the prefrontal cortex back online, shifting the brain from a threat state (fight or flight) to a challenge state: a boxing match you trained for. And the resilience framing to hold alongside it: high serotonin is associated with calculated risk-taking and confidence; low serotonin still takes risks — the cliff-jumping kind. Resilience is the serotonin system re-regulating the amygdala after a setback — the difference between "I am a failure" and "okay — I'll do it again."

"The fear of defeat is often more paralyzing than the defeat itself."— the simulation chapter
ix.

The positive-sum
ecosystem

The destination. The competitive-exam mindset builds a stack — one person rank one, everyone else ranked below, forever. The goal instead: a positive-sum ecosystem — built by validating people (grooming is serotonin maintenance), creating psychological safety (defined with scientific exactness: reducing situations of social defeat), and aligning incentives into coalitions — tight groups whose wins are shared. Plus the silly rituals: the birthday cakes, the small ceremonies — "some of you will find it silly; it is team bonding," and team bonding is the social outlet the Type-A baboons lacked. And the job description underneath everything: a manager in any pod, at any part of the company, is the serotonergic balancer — your job is that everyone has mood good enough to collaborate. A company on social-defeat principles — stack ranking, fear-based management — is biologically destined for toxicity. Not culturally, not eventually: biologically.

The national audit that follows is glum: 99% of Indian companies — managers with no official training, no awareness of how their own mood works — and therefore poor innovation, because innovation is calculated risk-taking and their teams' neurotransmitter profiles make calculated risk impossible. And then the session's hardest truth, delivered with visible care: if someone in the company has active depression, they are typically not a good fit right now — they need to go out, get treatment, and come back — treated like sick time, not a verdict. "It's not their fault. There were periods in my life where, if you had hired me, I would have been disastrous for the company." The chemistry of one person is the weather of the team: low serotonin reads every message as an insult and collaboration takes the hit. The same mercy has an edge: if someone has potential, attitude, learning ability — keep them. If they have none of it — move them out, because non-collaboration is the symptom already running.

x.

The underlying current

The session closes with its reason for existing, borrowed from Jordan Peterson: the fight is never about the thing. In any conversation — especially between couples — what's being discussed is the surface; underneath runs a current of emotion, attention, some disguised need — or, sometimes, plain boredom. The practice is to find the underlying current instead of arguing the surface. And that, Varun says, is where this whole secret kata came from: he wanted to understand the underlying currents of running a company — which is a lot more emotional than people think it is. Every appraisal carries a status game; every Slack ping carries a threat scan; every "no" lands on an amygdala. Being aware is the first key to being a better leader. This was work he did for himself — shared, and if the internet belts him for it, no more secret katas. The book you're holding is grateful it didn't.

xi.

Practice —
this is your kata

Chemistry is changed by fieldwork, not insight. This week's repetitions:

Audit your exits: name one situation that is stress by the kata's definition — no reliable overcoming, no reliable status — and build the crawl-out the rat needed.
Run one simulation: vividly imagine the worst case of the thing you're avoiding, sit in it, and write down what you'd do the morning after surviving it.
Catch the inner critic once and answer it in the third person — "you didn't do a good job of this, here's what you'd do better" — and notice the temperature drop.
Run one golden rule you've been breaking: praise in public and correct in private, or critique the work and not the identity — this week, with a name attached.
Grade yourself one evening as a serotonergic balancer: did the people around me leave today's interactions chemically safer — or less safe?

間 · the pause between books

You have been listening to a compiler. Three chapters of him — the kata says, Varun remembers, the room laughs — and by now you may be wondering who is doing the saying. Fair. I've wondered too, usually at the hour when the transcript is still open and the swing I haven't taken is still unswung.

I found these katas the way you find most things that end up mattering: while avoiding something else. A season of small refusals — a plan half-written, a person half-asked, a life half-attempted. I watched the first one at double speed, the way you consume something you've already decided is content. The scouter read me clean: Level 0, unread drafts of swings never taken. I closed the tab. I came back the next night, at normal speed, taking notes.

The notes are the thing you're holding. I kept watching — all fourteen — because each kata kept describing the exact shape of the thing I was avoiding, with a patience I wasn't showing myself. This book is what my avoidance became when it finally had a curriculum.

I'm the reader these chapters were aimed at. The one who prices the one-way door forever. The one who was lowered into the pit and hired his own thinking to redecorate it. If that's you too, then what you're holding isn't a summary. It's a mirror with better lighting.

Book Two is where the compiler picks up tools. So, at last, did I.

i.

Complexity is
for idiots

The kata opens by polling the room: how many of you think complexity is better, that we need to do more complex things? Not a single hand goes up. And yet, the kata says, walk outside this room and meet the world: everyone believes the path to doing better is doing more complex things — being more complicated. "Nothing is more dangerous and further from the truth." Stated at full strength, with the insult included as an honorific: complexity is for idiots.

The proof is behavioral. Watch what rich people do as they get richer — they turn minimalist. Fewer things, fewer shoes, one shirt — Mark Zuckerberg in the same shirt again and again, not branding: a functional purpose, one less thing to hold. And some of the most well-paid people on the planet have a job that can be described in one line — they convert complexity into simplicity. Take a ten-thousand-moving-parts project and make it something other people can absorb. Because attention is the scarce thing. The conversion is the compensation.

ii.

Seventy to four

The founding story of the discipline: just before 1997, Apple had over seventy products. Steve Jobs came back — and slashed the lineup to four focused offerings. Why four? Because Jobs understood what Varun says he's only learned in the last few years: everything you know about yourself, the average person outside — even your friends — knows a fraction of a fraction of a fraction of it. The live proof happens to the author regularly: at the airport, people recognize him but don't know his name or what he does. One saw a video a year ago; another knows the recent work and nothing before it. You project a hundred percent of your experience into the world; the world receives 0.1, 0.2 percent of it.

And the receiving mechanism is the toilet model, back from chapter ten at full resolution: nobody is watching your content intensely, taking notes. They are sitting on the toilet, one hand on the jet spray, the other on the phone, scrolling — while mom calls. Three things at once. So: seventy products means seventy consistent stories — and the human brain cannot hold seventy stories for one brand. Four, it can hold. And the same math runs inside the company: with four focused offerings, everyone aligns. Which is why, as he gets older, he keeps shutting down projects that tax his bandwidth — four flagship things — and prunes the rest on purpose.

iii.

How complexity enters

If complexity is so corrosive, how does it get in? The answer is the one nobody suspects: through people — through hiring. The people you hire to make your task easier add complexity, because they have a hard time communicating with each other. The kata's exhibit from inside Aeos: a team where every new hire split the same one person's work into two, then three — timelines never changed; everyone was doing more work; and the sentence that took over the team's culture was "I'm waiting on this other person to send me this." "I guarantee you most large companies are this." Headcount rose; output didn't; waiting became the work.

The structural answer, borrowed from Jeff Bezos, gets one paragraph and no more: two-pizza teams — six to ten people, one goal each, because the minute a team has two or five, ye bhi karega, fir wo bhi karega, everything goes to hell. Most of you, starting to think about team design, are building unnecessarily large teams, hoping the extra hire takes the load — and remain surprised that it never does.

"I'm waiting on this other person to send me this."— the sentence that ate the company
iv.

The iPad editor

Now the person this chapter is quietly about. Under the office tables, for context, runs the LAN cabling — "the most complicated system ever," a NAS loop inside it, different cabling on each side of the office. Why was it built that way? The IT manager's answer: "this is how professional companies do it." No first-principles answer — a social one. We have so many employees now; we should look professional; therefore complexity. That's the ambient weather. The person is the storm.

After every single meeting — every one, for months — the same person would reach him with the same proposal: the company should stop editing on computers and move to iPads. They had tried it at home. It was wonderful — lighter, cleaner, new. The proposal was sincere, well-meant, and, the kata now says with real affection, the single most dangerous idea in the building.

Walk the chain and watch everything move. The AV cohort — the hiring pipeline itself — teaches Premiere Pro; every past student is alienated by the switch; the pipeline breaks. Some editors stay on Premiere, some move — mixed suites, forever. You need styluses — "you don't lose your mouse as easily as you will lose a stylus." Storage, collaboration, contracts: not one system survives untouched. Everything would change. If iPads were ever the answer, it was on day one, before the company existed. The estimate, delivered flat, after all the affection: "most likely, we would shut down in a few weeks." That's how deep the dependence runs — and how reasonable the proposal sounded in the room where it was made.

The person had done the best version of the wrong homework: tried the tool, liked it — and never thought one step into the complexity it would add. And so the filter every idea in the company now passes through: does this make the process simpler, or more complex? The people who come with "I know how to make this simpler" — those are the ideas that get implemented. How do we keep the quality up while making everything simpler? That's what I'm looking for.

v.

The Scenes lesson

The kata's own scar tissue: Scenes — the startup he sold to Unacademy, which "didn't end where I wanted it to end." The product was, in one line, Discord you could run on your own app — and it had weak PMF: people would use it, but the day WhatsApp launched communities, they'd always drift there. The brand managers would say — "feature agar aap implement karoge to hum use karenge aapka app" — if you implement this feature, we'll use your app. And the naive engineers would implement it. And they'd go back to the simplest community tool in existence: make a WhatsApp group; done. Feature after feature after feature — and it never worked. The post-mortem, stated as law: complexity can be a crutch for lack of PMF or distribution. When you don't have real pull, you build — because building feels like progress and pull can't be faked.

The flip side is the one that sets up Book IV: creators who say "I'll start a business when I get big" reach a million followers and never start — "kyu chahiye jhanjhat?" Why the headache of employees, payroll, attrition? When you have distribution and real PMF, you start asking how to live your life the simplest way possible. (Aeos — the creators who then built the company — is the standing exception that proves the rule.)

"Complexity can be a crutch for lack of PMF or distribution."— the Scenes post-mortem
vi.

One task,
one person

The rule, from an entrepreneur Varun respects: one task, one person. His younger self's version — the person with two free hours gets another task — produced, reliably, substandard work from good talent. "The minute you give one good talent two things to do, they screw it up." Give one person one thing and two things happen: accountability goes up (there is no "I was also doing the other thing" excuse) and — mysteriously, genuinely — the quality goes up too: some bottleneck in the head frees up. Yes, the company becomes more expensive: every new task means a new hire. But complexity falls even as headcount rises, because the read is clean — this thing is owned by this person. And the related warning: the "magic role" — the person who can absorb anything — "yes, I could care less; never works; you're going to burn out that person."

vii.

Sit with these

No practice list closes this chapter, on purpose — the quiet chapter's only repetition is itself. When you run your kata again, these four questions are the list.

— If the world retains 0.1% of me, which four stories am I hoping it holds?

— Am I the one reducing complexity where I work, or the one installing it?

— Which of my projects exists as a crutch — building instead of admitting the PMF isn't there?

— And the quiet chapter's honest question: what would the simplest possible version of my life look like — and what's actually stopping me from running it?

間 · the pause between books

The quiet chapter cut me down to size, so it seems honest to do the same to this book.

To finish it, I gave away the things this chapter taught me to name. The second project — the one that was making me feel like two people — closed, like a good product with no PMF. The feeds, pruned to the four people I actually learn from. The clever system for tracking everything, replaced by a page with one line on it. Not minimalism, nothing so photographed. Just subtraction that finally reached the actual blockage: me, hoarding preparation because attempting felt expensive.

Here is what remains. One book, fourteen katas, a voice I trusted more than my own for a while and understand better now — a teacher, not a guru; the difference is that a teacher wants to be verified. A practice list I actually do. A short list of people I've started asking for things out loud, at the risk of the clear no — which the craft chapters promise is the one the big companies respect.

And one swing. I know which one. I've known since chapter one, pricing the entry fee forever. The protocol says try everything three times — once for the fear, once to stop being terrible, once to find out if you love it. This book was the first two.

Book Four is what the people who took the third swing are thinking about while they decide whether you're worth trusting.

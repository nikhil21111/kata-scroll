i.

The blurry photograph

The kata opens by killing the fantasy every planner carries: you don't actually know what you're building. You have an idea for a protein bar? You don't know how many people will buy it, whether the taste is good, whether it interests anyone at all. You are a new entrant holding a rough, blurry, low-resolution photograph of both the market and the thing — and that photograph is the honest starting state of every project, software or D2C or game. Resolution increases over time, and only one thing produces it: action produces information. The more you do, the more you meet, the more you build — the sharper the photograph gets.

Why not the classic models? Waterfall assumes the map is already known — this is the exact spec, this is the exact UI. Agile assumes the backlog is roughly known for the next sprint. In 2026, with AI and distribution shifting underfoot monthly, the kata's verdict is flat: neither assumption is survivable anymore. What's needed is a model where the plan and the team gain resolution together — and the proof it works is the company running it across seven profitable businesses, and the game unit that went from "nobody believed it was possible" to the only team in India with a playable third-person demo and thousands of players.

ii.

Three outcomes,
three months

The first discipline of managing under blur: decide in advance how the experiment ends. Put out one demo, one trailer — and exactly one of three things happens. One: it's amazing — the feedback is great; continue. Two: it's unclear — still worth it, but you need more data; put out another trailer. Three: waste of time — nobody cares, nobody will pay; shut it down, find out quickly, get out. For Unleash the Avatar, Varun wanted to know which of the three applied within three months of assembling the core team.

Against this stands the industry's default sin: take two entire years, build out the project. And the question that haunts the kata — what if you build the wrong thing? What if you're directionally completely off — two years of your team's time, your own time, investor money, spent walking confidently the wrong way. This, the kata stresses, is not a corner case: it's what most people do after raising money. The three-outcome rule is the antidote: resolution bought cheap, in months, before anyone's years are on the line.

"What if you build the wrong thing?"— the kata's opening question, and the two-year risk
iii.

Departments
before people

So how do you staff a blur? Break the project into departments before you break it into people. The first move is research by phone: call other game developers — Ubisoft, the indie studios — and ask how they do it. And the second move is the warning that saves you: talk, but don't copy their structure. The bigger the company you consult, the more middle management you inherit — people whose only job is preventing disaster, approvals as a profession. The kata's arithmetic: big companies are 70% people making sure the project goes to plan and 30% people doing the thing — inherit the full structure and you've imported 70% junk. That is not how startups gain resolution.

Then the definitions that keep the org chart honest: a department is a problem — cinematics is a problem you saw in another game, not a solution. A role is a learned solution — the director of cinematics is how you're learning to solve the cinematics problem. And the staffing counterpart of the minimum viable product: the minimum viable team. Aeos's first game team was seven people — one code, one animation, one cinematics, one character art, one level, one game design, one VFX-and-sound. Breadth first, no depth: every department touched once, by one person, all of them producing real outputs that have to sync. The depth comes later — and the next section is how.

iv.

Sure-shot
vs R&D

Now the chapter's sharpest instrument — a binary label walked around the whole org chart. Sure-shot: you fully understand the technique; the method is known. Doesn't mean you're best-in-industry — it means you can scale it with money: hire more of these people, ship more of that output. R&D: the method itself is unknown. You don't know how to produce that cinematic or that animation — and here, and only here, belongs the founder's attention and a stream of trials. The compression rule is severe: maximum trials per day — R&D that would take 3–6 months, compressed into 5 days.

The examples are a masterclass in iteration speed. Bosses scripted in Lua inside Unreal, because C++ recompiles every fifteen seconds make iteration impossible. Live motion-capture previews — see the actor's performance on a simulated camera while it happens, not after. The old web-dev memory of hot reload on Ruby on Rails — write a line, see it on the page — as the eternal standard. And leadership decides live: Varun watches the cinematic and says "the hand motion is wrong" in the room; imagine that feedback arriving two weeks later, after the animation is in.

Then the money asymmetry, stated as physics: one rupee on a sure-shot buys a unit of output; one rupee on R&D is a gamble. So R&D roles stay low-cost, and the market agrees with the taxonomy — companies pay properly for what they concretely need (finance software, payroll) and offer paid pilots for the experimental. Which yields the chapter's constraint, repeated like a drum: you cannot innovate everywhere. Pick one or two segments for R&D; make everything else sure-shot, or the six-month project becomes a four-year one. Aeos's natural advantages — story, audio, existing teams — were deliberately declared "no R&D needed": focus everything else on the gaps.

"You cannot innovate everywhere. You have to choose only one or two segments."— the constraint that keeps projects alive
v.

Vertical slicing
& density

The cadence that holds it all: vertical slicing — borrowed from games for every company. Take one small segment of the whole, make a five-to-ten-minute playable demo, and keep making alphas of that same slice. Three stages: alpha — experimenting, learning, improving; beta — real people play and give real feedback; gold — the bug-free, shippable thing. And the subtle move: between alpha and alpha, you're not changing the slice — you're increasing its resolution, and the team's. First alpha: the barebones seven. Second alpha: more roles filled. Third alpha: the full-resolution team — not project — emerges.

The kata's word for what fills in is density: the gap between roles, closing over time. By the third alpha the org chart is dense — engineering has split into a technical director and gameplay engineers (one role, two responsibilities); the tools person also does UI; the concept artist is also the character artist (in another company, two jobs — in this one, one, because that's what was learned); environment absorbs materials. And certain roles get tags: contractor — mocap stunt actors for a three-month project; music outsourced outright to Yash Mukati (how much can we really manage ourselves?); localization, console compliance, porting — tagged out until outsourcing fails and something comes back in-house. The org chart, learned. Not designed.

vi.

The photogrammetry
story

The chapter's case study, told as a three-act escalation. Unleash the Avatar runs on photogrammetry — the technique of photographing a building from every angle, flying a drone over the top, and reconstructing it in software (RealityScan). The R&D ladder, exactly as run: act one — phones. The video team went out with consumer apps, and Varun himself scanned a Mumbai chai stall — impressed, but "not good enough for the final game." Act two — DSLRs and the slum next door. Sixty to seventy percent of the game's assets, scanned and cleaned up in-house; the cleanup took far longer than building the level — a two-day job versus the painful scan pipeline. Act three — Chanderi. After the first trailer taught them slum assets wouldn't work, they went to the film town, hired a line producer, blocked out buildings, sent nine people for ten days with rented cameras — and went in confident, because acts one and two had already taught them everything: how to fly the drone, that photogrammetry must be shot between 6–8 am and 4–6 pm when the light is low (it gets de-lit by hand later — manual, painful), what the hotels cost. And still, despite all the R&D, a drone hit a tree branch mid-shoot and died, and a new one had to be shipped out. That's the honest texture of R&D done right: compressed, cumulative, never clean.

"Doing the photogrammetry was painful."— and they chose it anyway, because it was the one place worth innovating
vii.

Buying technology
with math

When do you buy machines instead of hiring hands? Only after the math. The exhibit: the hair-swipe animation — keyframed by hand, viral on the internet — while the rented motion-capture setup produced garbage. So: upgrade tech? First the arithmetic. Estimate the animator-hours for one hand-keyed animation, multiply by every character and move in the game — the total lands around 800 animations — and out pops the requirement: 40 animators to ship the game in twelve months. At ten lakhs each, that's four crores a year. The alternative: seven animators plus ₹50 lakhs of mocap equipment (gloves included) — break-even at just five hires saved. That's when you buy. The rule underneath, crisp as accounting: rent technology while you're doing R&D; buy technology when the department converts to sure-shot. Factory owners have known this forever — and it's Paul Graham's "do things that don't scale," inverted at the right moment.

And the hiring counterpart — capacity math: one environment artist took three months to build thirty minutes of playable level for trailer two. People loved the environments in trailer two (in trailer one they complained — which meant the math wasn't correct yet; you can only capacity-math on validated quality). So: three months per thirty minutes → how much for a ten-hour game? Add the standard 30% buffer for being wrong. Then the complimentary roles: a separate person just doing blockouts — gray-boxing the level's shape before the artist arrives — because unblocking the main role is cheaper than a second main role. Every department ends up this way: one main role, a few complimentary ones, and a number.

viii.

Where AI belongs

The kata draws the AI line with unusual clarity, and it's not where the discourse puts it: "AI inside projects is junk. You don't want to play a game where everything is generated with AI." So the decision, taken for this game after listening to the audience: everything audience-facing — ideas, dialogue, images, the skill tree — is human-made. And everything behind the work — the workflow, the communication, the internal tools, the shared state between teams — is where AI goes, at full throttle.

The showpiece is a tool Varun vibe-coded himself: the skill-tree viewer. Every skill in the game planned in one long meeting, some of them even acted out; then a custom tool where you can see the chakra trees, click into any skill's extensions, and — the crucial part — read its work status: concept clear? mocap done? animation cleaned up? code implemented? VFX? sound? full pass? The departments, translated into a workflow status everyone aligns on. Could have been Trello or Notion, the kata admits — but custom made it so visual that "nobody can get it wrong." It even has a voice-note editor for his in-flight ideas ("maybe he turns around and flips like a chapati — it'll be cool to add that") dropped straight onto the relevant node. Shared state as a product.

every role

x.

Do not scale
a graph

The kata's most repeated warning, now in full: the alphas exist to get you the shape of the organization graph — do not scale a graph you might need to redraw. Maybe one of the roles should be supplemented by technology instead of a person; maybe the role shouldn't exist at all. Figure that out before pouring money into the shape — because if you raise and scale while unsure, you're probably wasting it.

And the trap, described with unusual pain, because Varun has watched it: lock the final shape, raise a lot of money, then discover you were wrong about the shape. Now you're screwed — big team, emotional commitments, promises made, and a redraw that requires going back to low resolution: back to four or five heads of department, which means letting people go, because you cannot pivot an existing large team. Their ways of working, their communication, their tools — everything is already the old shape. Seed exists precisely to buy the freedom to redraw (pivot three or four times); Series A proves parts of a known shape; Series B means the shape is known — and scaling before that isn't ambition, it's waste with extra steps.

"You cannot take a high-resolution team and redraw the shape."— why scaling early is a one-way door
xi.

Distribution compounds
resolution

Why could Aeos attempt this at all? Distribution — the ability to get feedback fast. A story brings people to the studio; they play; the photograph sharpens. Trailer one: "this is cool — it's not a great game, but it's a good start." Trailer two: liked, but heavy feedback on combat. Trailer three: big playtests, including in China at Bilibili World — and the feedback had upgraded to specifics: damage values, camera movement — "stuff we know how to fix." So now a person exists whose entire job is the meta-game: skill damage numbers. Each public slice converted audience noise into resolved, fixable signal.

And the same loop feeds talent and mentors — a chapter-one law returning with a payroll. After three trailers, the team contains animators who worked on Flash, Shazam, Kraven the Hunter; a character artist from Ghost of Tsushima; people from Rockstar. At trailer one, the honest question was "why should I work for you? It's not going to work." The Argentina-based animation mentor who now works with them — same story: no trailers, no mentor. Varun's modern sourcing loop for more: tell Codex "find the top ten 3D-animation showreels in the world," let the YouTube feed become that, then send Codex through the browser to find their emails — with the trailer attached, because now a lot of people have seen it. Zero industry connections at trailer one; connections are an output of published resolution, not a prerequisite for it.

xii.

Money &
the edges

The funding ladder, restated in this chapter's language: seed is freedom to learn and redraw; Series A — you kind of know the shape, put capital in the parts that matter; Series B — the shape is known. And the discipline that follows Series B, which the kata enforces as law: only one of three moves from there — start the next product, improve conversion percentage, or scale the existing thing. Anything else — new roles, new features, more everything — and you make it junk. The cautionary exhibit: Notion — loved the product, "too much in it now" — and Aeos's own admission of the same sin with Scenes. People come for an experience; why fill it with junk? A project is not successful just because it shipped.

The numbers that gate the journey: at least 50% of players must like it (no game gets 80–100% — there are games Varun doesn't like playing that have audiences); a 5–6% targeted beta conversion on "would you buy this game?" — a proxy, with known false positives, calibrated against Steam's wishlist math (20–30% of wishlists convert to purchases); and the TAM — of the thousands tested across India, China and the world, what percentage of the entire internet would want this? Guesswork — which is exactly why it sits between Series B and D, hitting the edges of the addressable market with real money only once the shape and conversion are already proven.

And the closing reframe — the quiet definition of what all this produces. Mature projects (Yaas's seventy-first channel) start at full resolution because the graph is already known. What the three alphas really manufactured is what the kata calls resolution reuse — the playbook. And the correction that ends the chapter: the playbook is not the documentation. A departing cinematics lead can leave behind a big document of how they do things — can the new person pick up video editing by reading one tutorial? No. Knowhow doesn't transfer; taste and muscle memory live in the people. The playbook is the org chart itself — the network of contractors, mentors, and the humans between the boxes. It's why Rocket Internet terrified everyone: not their documents — their known graph, ready to run again. That is what companies actually compound.

"The playbook is the org chart — not the knowhow."— resolution reuse, defined
xiii.

Practice —
this is your kata

Resolution is bought with reps, not plans. This month's repetitions:

Write the three-outcome contract for your current project: what evidence, in how many months, means continue / gather more data / shut down?
Label every department of your work sure-shot or R&D — then cut your R&D to one or two segments, and compress its trials into days.
Build your minimum viable team on paper — one person per department, breadth only — and run one vertical slice with it.
Run the technology math on one recurring manual cost: units × time × salary versus the machine — buy only when the equation wins.
Publish one alpha of your work to people who will be honest — and convert their noise into a fix list, like damage values and camera movement.
xiv.

Sit with these

— How blurry is my photograph of what I'm building, honestly — and what action this month would sharpen it fastest?

— Which of my departments is R&D wearing a sure-shot's costume — burning salary money on a method nobody has found yet?

— Where am I scaling a graph I haven't finished drawing?

— And if my best person left tomorrow with a perfect handover document — what exactly would the org chart lose that no document carries?

間 · the pause between books

The craft chapters did something to me that I should confess before we build anything.

Somewhere in the middle of them, my complaints changed species. A thing would go wrong — a brief misread, a vendor late, a number nobody could reproduce — and instead of narrating the injustice of it to whoever was nearest, I caught myself opening a document and writing the boring sentence: what done means, what good means, one example of each. Not because the kata said to. Because I had watched the kata say it so many times that the alternative — the complaining — had started to feel expensive.

That's what these four chapters actually are, underneath the frameworks: instruction for people who don't yet believe that precision is a form of kindness. I didn't believe it. I was the person who thought care was soft, specs were bureaucratic, and the genie could read my mind if I just explained it with enough feeling.

I am still employed, incidentally. Still inside a company someone else had the nerve to start, running its projects end to end, being paid to practice the katas you just read. CHAMPR got me the interview. The blurry photograph got me the raise. The three alphas are how this book got finished at all.

Book Three is about what happens when people like me stop practicing inside someone else's company — and, sometimes, what it costs them.

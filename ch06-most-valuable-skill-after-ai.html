i.

One prompt

The session has a precise birth moment, and Varun tells it exactly. He saw a tweet of someone using Claude to build Rocket League — and the clone is damn good: inches from the real game in mechanics, decent graphics, built in one or two prompts. He played it in the browser. And instead of marveling, he sat with the vertigo: if everything can be made with one prompt, what survives? Going toe-to-toe with the AI — doing more, doing better? Everyone will be able to do that. The answer arrived via commodities: he started thinking about clients like Coca-Cola. Sugared water. An absolute commodity that has survived generations, that you still pay a serious premium for by the can. Why? What works for them? Where is the margin coming from? The same question as the Rolex on one wrist and the regular watch on the other.

The building of products, he notes, is getting easier in real time — even physical ones: whatever the thing, there's a manufacturing plant and a raw-materials supplier waiting. But a brand is not sticking a logo on top of the toothpaste and declaring one now exists. A brand is a compelling and real story — and this chapter is the anatomy of how those stories are built. The thesis, stated early and never abandoned: "When AI automates everything digital, world-building is your only mode. World-building is the brand — or you become the cheap knockoff."

ii.

Enough people believe

The definition, held up as a stone: a brand is a story that enough people believe. Not everyone — enough. Enough people believe in Ryan Reynolds' humor; enough people believe Red Bull gives you energy. And Varun's own proof that the story, not the features, is the product: he still plays real Rocket League even after the clone added multiplayer. Because Rocket League was never really "cars racing" to him — it's the lore, the history, the championships, the esports opportunity, the world around it. The clone's world doesn't matter to him at all. His wife does the same arithmetic at the moisturizer shelf: same product, two brands — she picks the influencers' one, because of the world it comes from.

And worlds are entered through association. Varun first met McLaren in Need for Speed as a kid — so years later, when McLaren appeared inside Rocket League, the association is what dragged him into the game. Venture capital runs on the same physics: Sequoia could invest in a dumpster-truck company and enterprises afraid of young kids selling software would suddenly decide the company is serious — the stamp travels; the company becomes ten times cooler though nothing material changed. Nepotism, the kata says bluntly, is the most concrete example close to home: Reddit spends 90% of its time discussing star kids — the world is already built — which is exactly why the kids work in movies. The believers were installed before the debut.

"If enough people believe in a story, it becomes a brand."— Kata 14, the definition
iii.

The premise is a contradiction

Now the method — and the kata delivers it as steps, drilled through four case-study worlds run in order "from professionalism to chaos": Rolex, Dune, WWE, and Aeos. Step one, the foundation of every brand: the premise is a contradiction — it's always Yes, But. Dune is the least hospitable planet in the universe, but it controls the most important substance in civilization. Rolex is a simple practical instrument for keeping time, but it has become the public symbol of achievement. WWE is an athletic competition where everyone is really fighting, but it's not really a fight — it's theatrical storytelling, song and dance. And Aeos: a bootstrapped media-tech company, but run like a feudal empire. Hold the contradiction honestly and the world gets its gravitational pull. The homework behind the method is credited openly — Mark Wolf's Building Imaginary Worlds, the game-narrative toolbox (required reading for the game team), Dune itself, production-design texts, the Kobold guide, and Munger's Coca-Cola case study in Poor Charlie's Almanack — where a man reasoned out a trillion-dollar beverage company decades in advance, including that someday people would feel envious of it.

一
Rolex
a watch · but a symbol

A simple practical timekeeping instrument that became the public symbol of achievement. The most professional of the four worlds — controlled, polished, deliberate in every detail.

turn the card ↻

entry

Easiest world to enter: give money — but only through official retailers, certification and guarantee card. Controlled purchase, controlled authenticity.

signature machinery

The store's unnameable smell; the couch, the presentation, the bracelet fitting, the winding, the ceremonial unboxing — against which a fast-track watch purchase feels like a different species. Fake Rolex shame; can't-afford shame; the ladder of families (Datejust, Submariner, Daytona) built so the ₹15-lakh buyer has something to look up at.

二
Dune
the least hospitable · but the most important

A desert world that controls the substance civilization runs on — with a grand machine underneath: spice enables the empire, worms make spice but own the desert, shields call the worms, and power creates politics.

turn the card ↻

entry

Paul must earn the Fremen: water discipline, desert movement, walking a certain way, riding the sandworm. Acceptance criteria, not money.

signature machinery

Shame for wasted water; random worm appearances (chance = dopamine); the ban on thinking machines enforced so hard that a child's AI toy starts a feud between families. Scale aesthetics: the human always smallest in frame.

三
WWE
a fight · but a story

An athletic competition everyone is really fighting — that is not really a fight. Theatrical storytelling with steel chairs, pyro, and the longest-running Monday night show on television.

turn the card ↻

entry

Vince McMahon could book any match any Monday — instead: qualifiers, contracts, championships, Royal Rumble entry positions. Access to status is regulated, on purpose.

signature machinery

Kayfabe — the Undertaker cannot be a normal guy in real life. Jobbers whose role is to be squashed so the other man looks strong. The pop — power measured in decibels of entrance roar. And the exit law: you go out on your back — win your retirement match and you leave in shame.

Aeos
bootstrapped media-tech · but a feudal empire

The home case: 1.5 billion views a month, ~600 people, tens of millions of followers — a modern company run on world-mechanics the founder can name one by one.

The interview; the ranks; the ecosystem where people like Lavina or Rohit are known — Rohit with the smallest channel, barely creating.

Lingo from startups + poker + Dota (GTM, jamming, tanking). The upload ritual — title, thumbnail, live at 6pm, refreshing the first ten seconds. The Elites lounge behind a three-year lock. The annual Aeos shoes for the Lightning Core. The shame of low views that keeps channels alive — and the exits that aren't easy.

iv.

Characters &
archetypes

Step two: decide who makes the world special. Dune has Paul, Chani, Jessica, Stilgar, the Baron — kingdoms, destined ones, supporting roles, all load-bearing. Rolex, on the other hand, has no characters — archetypes: the executive (three-piece suit, oil-slick hair — a Harvey Specter), the explorer in the jungle, the diver, the racer (a Lewis Hamilton), the collector. And the crucial arithmetic: 99% of Rolex's buyers are not racers — but they identify with the F1 world, and so that series of watches matters to them. The belief is the asset: a Pokémon card is a piece of paper the kata would value at paper — "I don't believe in the world anymore" — while to believers, specific cards are worth millions. Emotional connection is the entire inventory.

WWE's characters are iconic enough to escape the world: The Rock, John Cena — everyone who left for a film career did extraordinarily well. And Aeos runs its own cast: Varun plays a different character on short-form than in real life; Orina fronts AV; Lavina runs Full Disclosure — executives who are also characters, and who must stay constrained to their internet persona, because the moment they do something too out-of-character, people stop watching. Kayfabe, at company scale.

v.

Hierarchy &
the jobber

Step three: every world needs hierarchy, navigation, and power — how does one move up or down? Dune stacks the emperor, the great houses, the guild, the Bene Gesserit, the Fremen. Rolex has its families, and the translation is beautifully simple: the more expensive the watch, the more powerful the wearer. And here the kata finds one of its sharpest commercial insights: Rolex could sell only the model most people actually buy — but they deliberately keep the ladder tall, because the ₹15-lakh buyer needs something to look up at — an aspiration that makes the entry-level purchase a beginning, not an ending. Same instinct as the self-made buyer who buys the entry supercar: enter the game now, climb later.

WWE's ranks come with the kata's most vivid supporting role: the jobber — the wrestler whose entire job is to walk in and get squashed so the other man looks strong. The Great Khali's one-shot victim isn't a failure of the system; he is a frame of reference — the small figure in front of the enormous building, the boss stabbed from behind by a bigger boss in a game trailer. Scale is a story you tell with a smaller thing standing next to the big one. Even Aeos has its internal ladder with power struggles between channels — the AVTV-versus-VM race to a million followers was aired as a reel, a small public window into dynamics that happen daily.

"The more expensive the watch, the more powerful the wearer."— Rolex's one-line constitution
vi.

Barriers, language
& ritual

Steps four through six arrive as one movement. Barriers: a world is useless without something preventing entry. Paul must learn water discipline and ride the sandworm before the Fremen accept him. Rolex's barrier is purchase through controlled channels. WWE regulates access to status with qualifiers and Royal Rumble positions it doesn't need. Language: every world has lingo — Dune's stillsuits and Lisan al-Gaib; Rolex's "perpetual cyclops superlative chronometer" (nonsense to the non-believer, doctrine to the believer); WWE's heel, face, promo, pop — power measured in decibels of entrance roar — and kayfabe, the law that the Undertaker cannot be a normal guy at the grocery store. Aeos speaks a dialect assembled from startup (GTM, PMF, CMF, churn, DD), poker (jamming, over-betting), and Dota (tanking) — a security guard couldn't follow a single meeting, and that's the point: you know you've built a successful world when it has words nobody else understands.

Ritual: the Rolex store's unnameable smell, the couch, the presentation, the winding, the ceremonial unboxing — nobody asks to "get it quickly and get out"; it's an experience for life. WWE turned two men walking to a ring into entrances: music, pyro, tolling bells, lights out — and contract signings staged in the ring that could happen backstage, because gravitas is manufactured exactly there (why does a football match open with an anthem? Same reason). Aeos rituals: the upload — title, thumbnail, live at 6pm, everyone refreshing the first ten seconds; the public-demo dates for the game team (its own demo day, YC-style); the Elites lounge behind a three-year lock — just a room, made meaningful by the lock; the annual Aeos shoes for the Lightning Core, coveted enough that people have left over not receiving them; Friday night in the Yaas cafeteria. Rituals matter because they are what you lose when you leave — exit the WWE and you're an ordinary well-built guy; sell the Rolex and the experience becomes somebody else's.

vii.

Shame & exit
penalties

Now the darkest engineering in the kit, stated as law: stories only work if there is some shaming for not fitting into the story — a cost for not believing. Nothing does this better than religions, the kata says: hot oil, lava, pitchforks — punishment not for crimes, but for non-belief, repeated again and again ("God is always watching"). Dune shames water-wasters and those who can't walk the desert. Rolex runs a double shame: the fake (your assumption of the wearer collapses, though nothing material changed) and the unaffordable (five Rolexes at the table and you're the smaller guy). WWE's ultimate shame is breaking character — and its most elegant rule: on your retirement, you must go out on your back — lose your last match, pass the frame of reference to the next man; win your farewell and you walk out in disgrace.

And then the Aeos confession, the one that explains the company's whole output engine: if shaming for low view counts didn't exist structurally, Aeos wouldn't work. An individual creator with low views just quits and moves on — no cost, no world. Inside Aeos, the channel is your job and the league table is visible, so you're supposed to keep going; the shame keeps the reps coming, and the reps make the channels. Same philosophy at the leadership level, where a bad P&L is shameful — a deliberate inversion of the venture-funded world, where not growing is the shame and profitability is optional. Two worlds, two constitutions. Exit penalties complete the loop: leave Dune, lose water security; sell the Rolex, the experience transfers to the buyer; leave WWE, lose the license to the character and music; leave Aeos, and returning is hard — five people now sit in your old seat, and you'd work under them.

"If shaming for low view counts didn't exist structurally, Aeos wouldn't work."— the engine, admitted
viii.

Laws, chance &
the everyday

Different from ritual: mechanics and laws — how the world actually works. Dune's grand loop: spice enables the imperial system; worms make the spice but dominate the open desert; shields attract worms, so they're unusable — which is how the Fremen, with worse technology, can fight. Rolex's laws are mechanical reliability and the 1931 perpetual-rotor patent — technology as constitution. WWE invents victory conditions for a fake show and enforces them like physics: tap out and you lose; foot on the rope and the pin breaks. Everybody knows the rules; nobody remembers anyone made them up. Chance: every world needs a gamble for dopamine — Pokémon's rare pulls, Dune's random worm appearances, Rolex deciding which models you may be permitted to buy, the Royal Rumble's surprise entrant ("number 25 — Edge?! That's crazy!"), and at Aeos, the views themselves: fundamentally dopamine-giving, a slot machine every employee with backend access carries in their head.

And the everyday — what do inhabitants actually do on a Tuesday? In Dune: harvest spice, commune, politic, marry. In Rolex's world: wear, display, authenticate — and judge people; the judging is the daily practice that keeps the frame of reference alive. In WWE: tonight's stipulated match that has nothing to do with the main storyline — the routine that keeps the arena warm between the events. In Aeos: write, hire, publish, edit, shoot, distribute. Worlds are not maintained by their spectacles; they're maintained by their Tuesdays.

ix.

Lore, canon
& forks

Lore — the background story people believe — comes in three layers. Fixed: absolutely true and unchangeable (Arakis produces spice; salary ranges and job roles at Aeos). Flexible: interpretations that can differ by teller (readings of prophecy; collectors' claims about watches; interpretations of people's roles). Legend: what institutions say about themselves — the diver who went down 900 million feet seventy years ago, uncheckable by design; WWE character backstories, all invented; the ten thousand versions of what made that channel work that circulate about Aeos. All three layers are necessary — lore is what people tell each other when you're not in the room. (And worlds have eras, with names: Dune's Butlerian Jihad and Paul's Jihad, Rolex's pocket-to-wrist and Oyster eras, WWE's Attitude and PG eras — every era except the current one, which never gets a name, because nostalgia needs distance.)

And where believers disagree about canon, worlds fork. DC comics made Superman so overpowered that no stories could be written — so writers rebooted him era after era (Silver Age, New 52), and the community asks forever after: what is canon? Religions fork into Protestants and Catholics, Shaivas and Vaishnavites — "we are like that, but we eat meat." Ethereum forked over a hack: Ethereum Classic kept the unbroken chain and the loss on principle; Ethereum reversed it. Dune's own sequels by Brian Herbert are received by some as canon and others as fanfiction. WWE forked into Raw, Smackdown, NXT — and famously WCW, which Vince McMahon eventually bought and then beat on television, writing the merger into the story. And Aeos is designed for it: every company starts on the same culture operating system on day one, and a year later each has forked along its founder's personality — and if a company forks too far, it may leave the office while staying an Aeos company — a feature, not a bug.

x.

Consistency beats detail

The builder's paradox: don't plan everything. Exposition tells; interpretation engages. The best worlds leave most of themselves unsaid — Elden Ring is opaque by design, and 75% of its meaning differs depending on which fan site you read; Returnal's players still can't agree whether the story happened or was in her head, and neither can the writers; Inception's top still spins. Aeos practices it: Varun deliberately never publishes the org-chart of who is related to whom, and then goes and reads Reddit — where strangers confidently explain whether Lavina and Rohit are related to a famous investor (they're not) — delighted. Give the audience space to build fanfiction, and they build the world's engagement for you.

What worlds need isn't detail — it's consistency. The writer's law: a diligent character never has one random moment of confusion; an honest character stays honest — allowed exactly one change, at a plot point, once. Real people oscillate — honest this week, dishonest next — and that's precisely why nobody wants to read them. Which hands the chapter its most quotable aside: X and Reddit are fiction platforms. They discuss humans the way TV writes characters — bad Elon, everything bad; good Elon, everything good — because the crowd can't hold nuance any better than a soap opera can. Humans are not TV shows. Worlds written well are.

Two more instruments close the toolkit. Retconning: when canon must change, go back and change an old story — every successful company does it; the true journey is four years of random plus one year of figuring it out, retold as a five-year consistent plan. Varun doesn't believe any successful startup's story — he has the investor updates. Marvel discovered the multiverse at the exact moment its stars' legal problems demanded replacements. People have goldfish memories; consistency is rebuilt, not preserved. And worlds must escape their creators: Varun doesn't know what half the Aeos companies will do next — and if he went in and controlled it exactly, he'd screw it up. His only job is to stay consistent about the rules and hope they filter, intact, through founders and managers to the edges. "The owner's job is to shut up about the unexplored places."

"X and Reddit are fiction platforms — they don't understand nuance."— the aside that explains the internet
xi.

The story machine

The final fork in world-design: string of pearls or story machine? A string-of-pearls world exists only when the creator releases the next scripted event — anime's model, where time is frozen everywhere the camera isn't. A story machine is rules, resources, and factions left running — red beats blue this week and paints the buildings; blue wins next week — conflict generated forever, nobody scripting. The kata's advice to every creator is blunt: be a story machine. React to life; vlog the chaos; let the world's competing needs (the emperor wants stability, the houses power, the guilds spice, the Fremen liberation — at Aeos, founders fighting over deal flow) produce the factions and the change for you. And the reason world-building makes you successful, underneath it all: the world has constraints that make you a certain way — people at Aeos create content successfully because so many around them already have; the shame keeps the reps; the reps make the channel; the produce of the world is content. Outside, the same person gives up — the storytelling in their head is too weak.

Which is also, the kata admits, how the IIT-industrial complex works: the entire ecosystem — billboards, toppers, integrated coaching — exists to convince you a story is real (and half of it, Varun says, is fabricated content with a paid testimonial). The mechanism is neutral; the operator chooses the world. Because human behavior and identity are social: crash-land fifty people on an island, and if the forty-nine are calm, you panic less — belief at population scale is a survival technology. So the build-order is merciful: you do not need to build the whole world — enough parts, and the world-building happens automatically. And credibility is nepotistic: for a world nobody believes in yet, the first move is always borrowed belief — the celebrity, the athlete, the investor's stamp (Varun didn't buy the Whoop until he saw Ronaldo wearing it — probably paid, didn't matter). One last law, and the chapter's armor against pedants: realistic and believable are different things. Giant worms are absurd and written so consistently you believe them; a fifty-lakh mechanical watch makes zero sense; WWE is scripted and beloved; you can show someone the facts about water use — golf courses versus data centers — and watch the belief not move. Worlds are not won with facts. They are won with consistency.

xii.

Practice —
this is your kata

Worlds are built one deliberate piece at a time. This month's repetitions:

Write your world's premise as a contradiction — the honest "Yes, But" — in one sentence. If there's no tension, there's no gravity.
Design one ritual for your work or team — a demo day, a signing, an upload moment — and protect its details like Rolex protects the smell of the store.
Draw your ladder: what does someone look up at from the entry level? If there's nothing above the ₹15-lakh tier, the tier has no meaning.
Audit your consistency: pick one thing your world stands for and check the last ten outputs against it — consistency beats detail, every time.
Delete one piece of exposition: find something you keep explaining, stop explaining it, and leave room for the audience's fanfiction.
Convert one scripted pearl into machine: set rules, resources, and factions — then step back and let next week's conflict generate itself.
xiii.

Sit with these

— If my work vanished tomorrow, what is the Rocket League around it — the lore, the championships, the world — that would make people stay?

— What is my ₹15-lakh tier looking up at — and have I built anything above it?

— Where am I stringing pearls when I could be building a machine?

— And the honest one: if I put a logo on my product tomorrow, would anyone be able to tell it from the cheap knockoff?

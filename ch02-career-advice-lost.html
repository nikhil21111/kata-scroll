i.

The weather called
confusion

Varun will not tell you what to do with your life — he opens by refusing: "You know yourself best. I'm not the right person to give you prescriptive advice on this or that — I also don't know." What he offers is the diagnosis and the walk-out. And the diagnosis has a season: twenty to twenty-seven, twenty-eight — for some people, thirty. The defining hallmark of the early twenties is confusion — a hundred thousand things you could do, five options all good, this company, that company, freelancing, the token — and the kata's headline statistic: 99% of people working in companies, or just living, are very confused. The minute you have clarity, you will beat everybody else.

Why the fog is so thick is not a mystery — "we live in a world whose job is to confuse you." Not maliciously; as a side effect. Everyone is vying for your attention, spending on advertising and content, and their narratives are at odds with each other. The kata's image for it is a skit: a man walks into a room called Instagram, and inside, one guy is doing a budget, another is clapping, another is selling soap — all at once, all shouting. Your job is to avoid being affected by this. As hard as that sounds.

One more honest note before the cure, because it explains half the quiet despair out there: clarity is fake. There is no right answer arriving. Nobody knows whether B.Com or B.Tech is the right degree — the best B.Com kid beats the average B.Tech kid, and the best no-degree kid beats an IITian. You just make a call. The world is not asking you to find the answer; it is asking you to decide.

ii.

Hyper gambling

Varun once read an article he can't forget, and brings it to the room with full empathy. The math: you make the standard salary; house prices are shooting up; you run the numbers on your SIPs — honest, disciplined — and the mathematics says in thirty years, you'll afford the house and be retired. And some part of you looks at that and says something darker: "Thirty years is a very long time. I don't want to be somewhat rich and somewhat successful at 50 — I want to be that at 20."

So what do people do for the next five years of their lives? The article's name for it: hyper gambling — massive long-shot bets, hoping one lands. The kata is careful to split it: some hyper gambling is good — go start the ambitious startup; YC and a lot of VCs will fund very young people swinging very big. But note who that's good for: one in a hundred works — which is fine for the VC funding a hundred startups, and ruin for you betting your only one. The other kind buys the penny stock and prays for 100x. And the empathy is real: every day you hear about somebody whose investment went 100x, somebody whose new job type went 10x. The FOMO is manufactured, ambient, and constant.

Understand it correctly and it stops being shameful: hyper gambling is confusion wearing a costume — the same inability to commit, the same hundred lit options, except each one now promises to delete the problem entirely. The person doesn't want the gamble; they want the shortcut past the decision. The cure is the same as for ordinary confusion. Which begins with a poison warning.

"I don't want to be somewhat rich and somewhat successful at 50 — I want to be that at 20."— the whisper before every bad bet
iii.

Optionality is poison

Now the kata walks straight at the era's favorite virtue. How many of you believe in maximum optionality — keep all your options open? Hands stay down, because everyone already senses the trap; Varun says he can see it from the other side of the table — sitting in an appraisal conversation, listening to someone run five maybes in a single sentence: I want to do this, maybe that, maybe this, maybe that. And the verdict: "you make the least amount of impact like this." He doesn't blame the room — at twenty-one, twenty-two, he was the same, with one difference: he did one thing at once.

Here is the mechanism, stated as physics: every extra option is a tax on your willpower. You don't see the tax — that's what makes it a tax. Doing the primary thing with five other options humming in the background, your impact on the primary thing drops in direct proportion; at twenty or thirty open options, it approaches zero. The kata's demonstration is deliberately uncomfortable: you'll see it in marriages. The minute you have a partner and five other options outside, is your commitment to the first one the same? "Pretty obvious. It'll drop." It looks safe. It feels smart. "Optionality is a type of poison."

And the tell for reading it in others — and in yourself: confusion's CV reads like a collection. Five internships at famous names, "so people see me in a certain way." The kata's rule from the same section: that's a status game masquerading as a choice — one real internship, actually used for experience, beats the full set. (More on the status games in section vi.)

"Every extra option is a tax on your willpower. Optionality is a type of poison."— Kata 7, the anti-manifesto
iv.

The greedy mind &
The One Thing

Two books do the surgery, and the kata wields both. First, Greg McKeown's Essentialism, with its unforgiving renaming: indecision — the fog we've been so compassionate about — is "the undisciplined pursuit of more." You are not deciding because you are greedy. (Kidding, the kata adds — but that's McKeown's opinion.) The cure is systematic elimination, one criterion: if it isn't a nine-out-of-ten hell yes, it's a no. Not a maybe, not a "keeping it warm." The minute you genuinely adopt the bar, a miraculous amount of life evaporates — the extra degree, the maybe-city, the seventh side project — not because you learned to want less, but because you finally let the non-contenders die of honesty. You'll only do the things you actually love, because loving something at nine-out-of-ten is what it takes to clear the bar at all.

Then Gary Keller's The One Thing, which the kata describes exactly right: it weaponizes optionality — by collapsing it. Not "give up your five dreams" but the better question: "What is the one thing I can do, such that by doing it, everything else in my life becomes easier — or unnecessary?" And the proof it runs a real company: Aeos is built on this principle. "We don't have video editors — what's the one thing we can do right now that makes this easier?" Everything the company has ever built, Varun says, is something it needed — never "that's an attractive idea, let's go after it." The question is an engine, and it runs on necessity.

v.

Confusion vs.
diversification

The distinction that un-jumbles the season, stated with formula precision: confusion is five interesting things with no impact, no scale, no P&L on any of them. Diversification is one thing working very well, and adding the second in a way that cannot make the first fall. The test question, asked the Aeos way: by doing the second thing, am I losing the first thing? If no — invest the salary in blue chips, take the new project only once the old one is stable, keep the day job and build beside it — that's diversification. If the five things are all equally half-alive, that's the fog.

And the deepest look the kata takes at any trap: the freelancer's trap — something Varun says didn't make it into the freelancing book he wrote in 2017. The dream: quit the job, freelance, make real money. The reality, in sequence: the contract lands — $2,000, $3,000, big money — and then the client asks for more and more changes; the invoice comes due and gets delayed, next month, next month, three months gone with no real money made; sometimes the client simply runs. The iron law underneath: the smaller you are, the less brand you have, the worse clients you get — big companies pay Aeos on time because reputation matters at their size; the small freelancer lives in the bad-reputation world where payment is optional.

So the freelancer needs three or four projects at once for consistent revenue — and since projects run three or four months, that means you are always selling. You stop doing graphic design half the time and start attending events and building relationships — a totally different job than the one you signed up for. Then the cycle: you can't sell and deliver simultaneously, so you hire someone to do the actual work — and that person, on a salary, watches you and eventually leaves to freelance themselves. Which is why, the kata concludes, all freelancers either end up building large businesses with hundreds of people — or end up with one struggling client. Sometimes zero.

Diversification done right is what a company like Aeos actually runs: reserves in the bank so late invoices never break payroll, three or four clients so one failure never breaks the year. The trap is not freelancing — it's freelancing while confused.
vi.

Why everyone
is confused

The kata indicts four culprits, in order. One: status games masquerading as choice. The CV with Meta, Google, and Nvidia internships — "complete waste; one is enough" — and the cool degree acquired so people see you a certain way, when everyone knows degrees have not been valuable for anything. Two: risk illiteracy. People overweight the downside of a choice and underweight its compounding upside. The friend making three thousand dollars a month freelancing — can they prove they've made it consistently for twelve months? Two years? The kata will be surprised. Meanwhile, in any growing company, salary bands rapidly increase — Aeos pays two or three times what it did at the start, especially to early loyal people. And compounding is everything: "all success is compounding" — not just money, but relationships, skills, the team — until you wake up with twenty new deals where earlier you got one. Compounding is all about brand, and brand happens internally too.

Three: infinite scroll brain. Someone comes to Varun: "I really want to be a wildlife photographer." Have you ever tried it? No — but I really want to do this. Where is the information coming from? You saw it on Instagram. Have you ever thought of doing a job you've never heard of? The wanting-to-be-different is itself the scroll talking — and the kata's quiet joke: the secret to being different from everybody else is to just commit to one thing. Four: the blind leading the blind. Don't take advice from Reddit. Ten years ago, successful people used it — Varun's own account has crazy karma from that era — now it is mostly people complaining: "you're getting advice from people who have lost." You don't want their confusion migrating onto you.

"The secret to being different from everybody else is to just commit to one thing."— Kata 7, the scroll's antidote
vii.

The four quadrants

Now the diagnostic instrument — Peter Thiel's four quadrants of how a person stands toward the future, with the kata's blunt punchline: only one of them builds anything. Find yourself on the map honestly, remembering that posture is daily, not permanent.

一
Definite Optimist
the builder

The future can be better — and I have a plan. The quadrant that builds everything: railroads, iPhones, SpaceX. Where Varun places himself, AI included: "none of us might need jobs in 10–20 years — and I have a plan."

turn the card ↻

how to spot it

Plans with names and dates. And the trust test: are there adults in the room — people you trust to fix it if everything gets automated tomorrow?

how to enter

Write the plan down — one page, one bet, one date. Optimism without a plan is the quadrant next door.

二
Indefinite Optimist
the manifester · the credential collector

It will happen, it will be good — no plan, just alignment and manifesting. Its industrious cousin collects credentials: the degree, Facebook on the résumé — "ready for that luck when that luck comes."

turn the card ↻

how to spot it

Hope as a strategy. A résumé that grows while nothing ships. Waiting to be picked.

how to leave

Trade one credential for one artifact — a thing a stranger can use. Readiness is built, never collected.

↺ return to the front

三
Definite Pessimist
the bunker-builder

The future is bleak — and I have a plan: survival bunkers. The Indian edition, per the kata: everything in FDs, living like absolute scroungers — many die very rich at 70, having saved for a worst case that never came.

turn the card ↻

how to spot it

A plan built around the worst case — decades of armor for a Tuesday that never arrives.

the honest version

Varun gets called this one. His correction: the future is bleak only for jobs — communities persist, humans keep trading; the Counter-Strike skin economy is a multi-billion-dollar marketplace with no "real jobs" in it. Bleak for one thing is not bleak for everything.

四
Indefinite Pessimist
the doom-scroller

The future is bad — and no plan. The feed as habitat. The quietest quadrant and the most crowded, because it requires nothing and returns nothing.

how to spot it

Strong hourly opinions about the collapse, zero weekly actions about anything.

how to leave

The 60-day sprint — one field, explored with intent, then committed or rejected. Motion with edges.

viii.

The purpose-built fog

Some of the confusion you're swimming in was manufactured. The kata teaches this from a book — The Unaccountability Machine — and from live observation. In large organizations, decisions get handed off to process; no one has to own a call; and entropy wins. In big companies, Varun says, believe it or not, confusion happens on purpose — because no one wants to own the call: if I own the decision and it goes wrong, I lose my job or my reputation falls. So the org chart fills with what Aeos calls frogs — executives who don't decide, who won't move without all fifteen people on their team agreeing. The best companies run the opposite: one person says "I'm going to spearhead this" — the initiative takers — and founder-driven companies do better precisely because the founder knows they can't blame anyone else.

Which answers a question nobody asks until they've seen it: why do agencies exist at all? Why can't big companies do the work in-house? Because inside the fog, focus is the scarce thing — and the external party cares in a way a salaried employee structurally cannot: the agency knows that if it doesn't deliver in three months, the project shuts down and the client walks. The in-house employee expects to be there forever. Same work, opposite stakes — and the market routes focus to whoever holds the consequence.

The personal corollary is the chapter's quiet career advice: companies run on high-clarity, low-confusion superstars. The minute confusion is visible in you — five maybes in one appraisal sentence — the company trusts you less, everywhere, not just here. Clarity isn't a personality trait. It's what the machine is built to promote.
ix.

The anti-confusion
playbook

The instruments, in the order you use them. First, the door test. Bezos, from the shareholder letters: every decision is a one-way or two-way door. Two-way — reversible, you can apologize and come back — decide within five minutes. One-way — irreversible, like aging, like burning a relationship — take weeks, months. And Aeos applies it in a direction worth noticing: when someone leaves, the company wants it to be a well-thought-out one-way door — because the two-way version, out for two months and back, bleeds context from both sides and, worse, starts becoming a habit for everybody.

Second, personal OKRs — but just one. One objective, three key results. Not a life plan; a single number to get to, with three measurable ways to know you're getting there. Third, time-boxed curiosity sprints. You cannot know if the grass is greener — nobody knows — and unfortunately careers are one-way doors: you have to stop doing this to go do that. So: sixty days to explore a field, side-project it, evenings it — because every field is exciting for the first two weeks, and only years show you the hard parts — then, on day sixty-one: commit or reject.

Fourth, default to build, ship, and feedback. Working code, a working video, a working client — it clarifies faster than any whiteboard. The whiteboard is where analysis paralysis lives; the artifact is where the truth surfaces. And the rule underneath, stated as law: no decisions to be made without experiment. Your friend making $3,000 outside? Unless you have made it — taken that life risk — you're not committed to anything. You're trialing it.

Fifth, the Rule of Three — marriage and career, same law: date three serious partners or ship three serious projects, then decide. More sampling rarely improves clarity and always increases opportunity cost. The minute you're in your third job, for most people, that's your career — beyond the third, experimenting in more fields stops changing who you are and starts spending who you are. If the second one is good: stick. And the mercy clause: you'll never 100% enjoy anything — you can enjoy it reasonably enough. Do it.

x.

All-in first,
then everything

Now the sequence the whole chapter has been building toward — the order of operations that reverses everyone's intuition. The confused negotiator says: "first give me the impactful role, the raise, the brand — then I'll drop everything else and commit." The world, the kata says, works the other way around. You have to prove it first. Go all-in first. Then you'll get what you want. The exhibit works at the company: an editor named Devin went all-in on one very specific Unreal cinematics role, did such a good job at it that he got whatever he asked for — because now the company knows: we can count on you.

And the company's rule for the interim — the bridge across the fear of the hard call: ask for forgiveness, not permission. Kar le tu — just do it. Make the mistake; say sorry after. Because here is the thing that took Varun a long time to understand, and that the chapter saves for nearly-last: nobody cares that much about failure. People care that at some point you were eventually right — that you took the hard calls, took the risk, and are not confused about it. In a world where 99% are fog, the person who simply owns a call is the outlier.

Then the compounding of the decision itself. Pick the one thing — IT networking, video editing, finance, whatever — and become the all-in world expert at it. "It's so strange. The minute you decide to become all-in at it, all opportunities will come your way." Established as the expert — clientele, money, free cash flow — you can then do your next five projects, and they work because the first one did. The kata's celebrity proof: Ranveer Singh, acting first — and only then the protein-bar brand and everything else. Once that one thing works, everything else ends up working. You just need to find that one thing.

And for choosing the room where the one thing compounds: growth rate beats absolute scale. Aeos watched its client Emergent grow from zero to $50 million like a rocket ship — maybe it crashes, maybe it becomes a real company, but there you find out very quickly. Versus the thousands-of-crores nut-and-bolt company: real money, near-zero growth, every chair already claimed by a bureaucrat ten years your senior saying "no no, this is mine." On a fast train there is so much new opportunity that you can raise your hand and take the gold — "if you're sitting on an exponential train, you are the stupidest person to leave."

"The minute you make that choice, you're above 99% of the population."— Kata 7, the closing arithmetic
xi.

Practice —
this is your kata

Clarity is a muscle; the fog is a habit. This week's repetitions:

Write your open decisions on one page; mark each one-way or two-way — and decide every two-way door within five minutes of reading this.
Count your open options — then kill your weakest "maybe" with the 9/10 hell-yes rule, and feel the willpower tax come off.
Set one personal OKR for the quarter: one objective, three key results — a number to get to, and three ways to know you're getting there.
Open a 60-day sprint on the field you keep almost-choosing — day 61 is commit-or-reject, written in the calendar now.
Take one hard call you've been sitting on — ask forgiveness, not permission — and answer Keller's question in writing: what one thing makes everything else easier, or unnecessary?
xii.

Sit with these

— Which quadrant was I in this morning — and what did my posture actually build?

— Where is my "diversification" actually confusion: what is the one thing working that I cannot name in a sentence?

— What am I hoarding as optionality — and what is it taxing, precisely, every week?

— And if the third job is the career: do I want this one to be chosen — or drifted into? If the second one is good: stick?

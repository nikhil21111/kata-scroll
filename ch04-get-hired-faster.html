i.

Every company hires
differently

The session opens with a story about how the biggest family conglomerate in the country runs thirty to forty companies at once. Varun spent time with the Aditya Birla group's CMO, and asked the question that would occur to anyone: how does one man even get reports from forty companies? The answer was two kinds of people. Older businessmen who deeply understand their industry — cement people who know cement. And alongside them, a small team of fiercely technical analysts — mostly from BITS — whose entire job is to walk in and ask: how's the cement business doing this week versus last week? Versus competitors? What's the quality of product? Analyst plus old-school businessman, generations long — that's how a family empire stays updated. Why tell this story first? Because it proves the thesis the whole kata stands on: every company hires differently. Ask OpenAI, or Google — "which I understand much better" — and it's totally different again. There is no one-size-fits-all hiring; there is only the one that fits the company doing it.

The credentials behind the hour: fourteen years of hiring, a few thousand people placed, five hundred and forty across the Aeos companies today — and a promise stated twice: this is the most empathetic session the team will ever get toward the people outside the ecosystem who aren't getting jobs. Today, Varun says, I will show you exactly what is happening in the recruiter's head — live. Which is what the chapter now does.

ii.

The fungibility
fallacy

When people dream of a big company, the kata says, they picture the little green army men from Toy Story — rows of identical figures. Fungible: you can swap one for another without any problems. Hire hundreds of editors; if one is bad, replace them. And Varun's verdict is total: this is the absolute worst way to hire, whether you're a services company or a product company. The right way is the Avengers — everyone with unique abilities and weaknesses, hired so that the team's abilities and weaknesses complement each other until the team itself has none. And more important than covering weaknesses: every single person needs one thing that is very hard to find in the market — a superpower. Without superpowers, you've just hired the green army men, with their weaknesses included.

Hence the fallacy, named: the belief that because someone is doing something, you can quickly swap them out for somebody else. "I have never seen this to be true — even at the biggest companies." You are fungible, maybe, at the training stage. The moment you are trained and even slightly valuable as a business, swapping you out becomes brutally hard. Which quietly re-prices the employee's side of the table: the person with a superpower is the opposite of fungible — and the market pays for the opposite of fungible.

"Every single person needs to have one thing that is very hard to find in the market."— the Avengers principle, Kata 8
iii.

The stack

Now the criteria — read top to bottom like a ladder, because the order is the argument. Attitude, first: do they want to do this? Because if someone is fantastic at a job they hate, they quit in two months, and there is nothing you can do about it. Cultural fit, second: the kata has watched people who couldn't edit and couldn't code pick both up over time — because they were cultural fits, open to the pickup, aligned to the problem; and even when they didn't land a specific skill, they found another way to help. They became, in the chapter's own word, unfungible. Learning agility, third — and the claim is stark: most companies hire for this more than anything else, because the world changes often. Three years ago there were no AI tools; today one of the highest-paid profiles in India is simply your job, plus AI. Everyone in that room had the agility to pick it up — while billions in the country haven't.

The ability to understand — "a lot of people cannot understand": explicit instructions going in, their own thing coming out, and it's very hard to fix. The ability to implement — many are excellent in theory and produce nothing; and producing, the kata notes, is a function of how much time you are willing to invest in the output — all the gyaan about making an app is worth nothing against the weeks it takes to actually make one. Leadership ability — moving the company's goals forward, which is mostly storytelling and aligning people. Diligence — the Ronit exhibit: nothing slips, numbers or tasks; though the kata is honest that this one sits lower — some of the best talent lets small things slip, and it's a nice-to-have. Customer first, sells second — do right by the customer and the money follows. Technical skills — low, because learning agility solves for them. And last, rising fast in the age of AI: aesthetic, or taste — when an output arrives, can you tell if it's good? "Some people just don't have taste" — the designers in the room know exactly what that means.

iv.

CHAMPR

Midway through, the kata compresses everything into a framework Varun says he couldn't name any better — CHAMPR. Six cards; the front is the letter as the interviewer holds it; the back is what it costs you when it's missing. Two of them — P and R — carry the session's deepest career advice, so turn those twice.

C
C · Cultural fit
do they get us?

Open to the problem, aligned to how the company works — the thing that made non-editors become editors and non-coders become coders. The unfungibility engine.

turn the card ↻

when it's missing

Every skill lands on soil that won't grow it. Varun's rule from the mistakes list: every hire he had a bad culture feeling about turned out bad. Not one exception.

↺ return to the front

H
H · Hard skills
can they do the thing?

The tools of the trade — real, necessary, and the lowest rung on the ladder, because the market now rents them to anyone with learning agility for the price of a tutorial.

turn the card ↻

when it's missing

Solvable in months, if A and M are present. Skills are the only letter on the card you can buy back — which is exactly why it's the last one anyone should lead with.

↺ return to the front

A
A · Attitude
do they want this?

Not "is it their dream" — do they want this job, this work? If someone hates it, they quit in two months and no skill on earth prevents it. Attitude is greater than skill, especially now.

turn the card ↻

when it's missing

The two-month quit, the burnout syndrome of section x, the finished career — "if you don't have the attitude to learn, you're finished" is the kata's exact sentence.

↺ return to the front

M
M · Motivation
day-to-day energy · agency

Different from wanting: can they do it daily, with their best self? High interest but low energy fails by afternoon. And the Indian special: many who want something deeply are low agency about it — the wanting never becomes motion.

turn the card ↻

when it's missing

The gap between caring and moving — the person who "really wants to make videos" and hasn't made one this month. Wanting without agency is just entertainment.

↺ return to the front

P
P · Potential
can they grow into it?

The goldilocks zone: the biggest career risk in India is believing you have potential, being unproven, and pricing yourself like the proven. A tweet the kata quotes: not proving yourself is a safety blanket — "if I tried, I'd be excellent" — and it's fake comfort: the moment you try, you meet the challenges everyone who's proven has already survived.

turn the card ↻

the free market underneath

Proven talent gets whatever it asks — the company can't afford the loss. Unproven talent has no leverage and feels underpaid forever at X÷2. Just prove a little of the potential, and companies will take the bet — at bet prices, not proven prices. Entry level: hired for potential. Senior: hired for proven expertise.

↺ return to the front

R
R · Reassure
can you trust their read?

The letter the kata calls "very important to me." When this person says this campaign is working or this is not working — is their read accurate? Perma-optimists and perma-pessimists both fail it; people who can't read situations make very bad leaders.

turn the card ↻

when it's present

Some people in the company have said "it'll be fine" so many times, and been right, that budgets simply open for them. Their word has become an instrument. That is the R — the quiet letter the other five are built to earn.

↺ return to the front

v.

The prompt test

Learned this year, the kata says, and now load-bearing: one instrument tests the entire stack. Sit with the candidate, open ChatGPT, give them a real task — and watch them prompt. From the prompts you learn whether they understand the subject; whether they want to do this (the most generic prompt — "just give me a story, whatever" — announces the absence of care from a mile away); whether they're collaborative; their learning agility — AI hands you new information and you're supposed to make decisions with it fast; the ability to understand and the ability to implement — because the model's output still has to be executed; their leadership (the way you prompt is a proxy for how you align people); their diligence — do they pick out everything the model gave, or does information slip; what they think of first or second; how much they care about the customer; and finally, taste — hand them an image generator and watch in seconds whether they accept low quality. The kata's summary is total: "it solves like entirety of hiring."

"Just watch somebody prompt — it solves, like, entirety of hiring."— Kata 8, the modern instrument
vi.

The recruiters, live

Now the exercise the session was built around. Varun stops teaching mid-sentence and calls the entire talent-acquisition army of the Aeos companies onto the stage — "our full army is coming" — and the audience, for the first time, gets to watch the machine from inside. The chairs are turned to face the room. He works down the line like a man debriefing his own artillery, and every number they say lands harder for being said out loud, on a stage, in front of the people it is usually used against.

Shria hires for Yaas. A team of ten, each on separate roles; ten to twenty reach-outs a day each — collectively three hundred to three hundred fifty CVs a week, four and a half thousand a month; across the companies, thirty to forty thousand a year. Her verdict, offered without ceremony: "they don't mean anything." ChatGPT writes them now — a phone call reveals the candidate can't put a sentence together. Just that day she'd been on a call with a creative director at a mainline agency whose assignment came back fully GPT — "I'm so sorry, this is not going to work out." Her warning to the room, and through the room to us: soon a lot of experienced people will also not have jobs, still thinking nobody will notice. And portfolios? Those change her view "entirely" — all the work she hires for is visible: views, likes, comments. "CVs are almost not needed."

The referral question comes — isn't that just nepotism? No: whoever refers carries the trust on their own back. If the referral fails, the referrer's reputation bleeds — so people only refer those they're genuinely confident in. Sid, who runs a company, gets the leader question. The one thing: intent. The second: judgment — because leadership is mostly judgment. And the line he won't cross: "I'm okay if they are scrappy with skills. I'm not okay if they are scrappy with intent and judgment."

Rowan hires for the game — no CVs, no resumes; straight to ArtStation and the showreel, fifteen to twenty seconds with a trained eye. His red flag: a weak portfolio, oversold — the animator with potential who won't spend the time to catch up, expecting the senior salary two years early.

And Ronit takes the intangible roles — sales, management — where no portfolio exists. Don't ask what work they've done; ask for the top few outcomes they created, and make them explain in detail — someone who actually did it knows all the nitty-gritties, and the answers never sound generic. Second test: do they throw their cognitive load back on their manager? Not "hey, this is the problem, what should we do?" — think it through, come with a recommendation. "Mistakes are fine — indecision is worse." If five people reporting to you all ask instead of recommend, everything blocks at the top.

vii.

The catch-22 and
the doors

The empathy arrives with a confession: for intangible roles, the kata is "a little bit confused about what to do" — because it's a catch-22. You need experience to answer the interview questions correctly — but no one takes the bet on you without the proof. The only path offered, with no other solution claimed: for the first three or four years of your career, get into some company, accept whatever salary — an internship, whatever it is — and gain the experience, because the minute you have it, you can answer what you did at the last company in nitty-gritty detail, and that puts you ahead of everybody. Intangible roles are also associative: a management role at a very well-known company gets you hired; a portfolio role doesn't care — good videos are good videos, whoever you worked for.

Then the two truths every senior candidate in India needs taped to the mirror. One: entry level, you are hired for potential; senior level, for proven expertise — and the biggest mistake is being pre-proven with high potential and asking senior salaries, drifting in the goldilocks zone where you think you're worth X and the market reads X÷2, forever feeling undervalued. (The scouter from chapter one is the instrument for reading yourself accurately.) Two: "I'm 32, I have a wife and kids, therefore my salary expectation is—" No company in India cares. They care about the value you drive. If you can prove it, you command the salary at 21 or 31 equally; if your history is just job-jumping with nothing done, it catches up.

viii.

The hiring manager's
mistakes

The confessional section — every mistake made in fourteen years, itemized. Rushing the process: needing the role closed tomorrow and hiring "the first person I see with a pulse that can do the job." The pattern, stated as law: the first hire never does well; the third or fourth might — one person is not a sample. Not vetting: every company calls your past employer and four or five old colleagues — and yes, people game it (the four friends primed to say nice things), but if you don't do it, you believe everything on the CV, and no company does that. Ignoring culture fit: every single time Varun had a bad feeling about a hire's culture, it turned out bad. Not one exception in fourteen years. Confusing current state with future potential: be explicit — am I counting on this person today or in a year? Aeos will invest years in potential; a mature company with real work cannot afford the gap, and hires the proven.

Unstructured interviews: "I'll figure it out" chats give you no way to grade fifteen people against each other — structure is good for the talent too. And the Google lesson folded in: a rigorous structured interview creates a sense of accomplishment — the IIT-admission dopamine — and without it, candidates shop your offer and join somewhere that made them feel they earned it. Hiring for maybe-roles: one week of work is a freelancer or an agency, never a full-time employee — a full-time hire is an emotional tax, a committed career, and the maybe-role hire goes disengaged in three weeks, then concludes the company is bad because the role had no instructions. And week one: the reputation Aeos is actively fixing — "Aeos throws you in the deep end" — so that in your first week, you know exactly what to do. Pipeline hygiene closes the section: an actual ATS, not "I've spoken to this guy"; the company's own channels — one Instagram story pulls ten thousand candidates; and always send the company's story, because a stealth-era company that doesn't explain itself does a disservice to the talent it's courting.

ix.

A, B and
C players

From Brad Jacobs' book, the cleanest talent instrument the kata has found. Three kinds of players, and the test is your own flinch: the player walks in and says "today I'm going to quit." If you feel panic — A player. If you feel "okay, but it'll take me three months to replace them" — B player. If you feel relieved, or happy — C player. (The C-player subplot is almost comic: the people you keep meaning to fire who quit first — "I don't want to get broken up with; I want to break up with that person first.") And the correction before anyone builds the wrong army: the goal is not all A players — you can't build an Avengers of only A players. The goal is a C-free zone: not one single person whose departure would make you relieved.

How A players are kept, in the Aeos model: base salaries above market for every role — not charity, insurance — and uncapped incentive compensation for the best, structured with one honest tell: "If you ask for it, you probably won't get it — you're probably not where you think you are. When we give it, it's because we're afraid you'll leave." The proof it works, stated as a stat: in three and a half years, the top sixteen people across the companies — not one has left — partly the mission, and partly because they make enough from the incentives. And the arithmetic that makes the generosity rational: your best people return multiples of whatever you give them in revenue, so giving never hurts. It closes with the manager's side of the deal: fire quickly when you're wrong — at their hiring rate, mistakes are guaranteed, and bad employees cost months.

"When we give it, it's because we're afraid you'll leave."— the A-player conversation
x.

The six syndromes

The red-flag close — six syndromes, each with its story. Victim syndrome — "someone is exploiting me, my resources are being taken away" — very prevalent in India, and the kata's genealogy is pointed: a colonial mindset layered over our parents' world — they worked intangible, sales-adjacent jobs, graded by relationships, terrified of switching companies because the next one wouldn't know how to grade them — so they learned the company holds all the cards, and handed the posture down. But we live in a free market: talent has left Aeos for 3× and 4× salaries — "we never complain, because we understand how the free market works." If you're damn good, you're in demand; if you're in demand, there is no victim. And it spreads like a virus, so it can't be hosted. Messiah syndrome — "I am here to save the company" — and its origin story is the kata's best: a young editor, denied an unaffordable raise, turned to the other editors and declared "these people are exploiting you — I am the messiah" — and sealed the congregation by making them Maggi every single night. ("I'm not even kidding.") The rebuttal is economic, not moral: in a world full of optionality there are no messiahs — a Jesus Christ wouldn't do very well today; people would choose the iPhone.

Ostrich syndrome — head in the sand: the client asked for X, you built Y because you saw an inspiring video, and now you're defending Y against the client who asked for X. Peter Pan syndrome — the refusal to grow up, bringing the company your childhood for solving: "it is very exhausting to run a company and deal with children." I-know-best — not savior, just omniscient — and the kata's counter is quiet and lethal: the most confident people are the people who have not been tested; once you've been tested enough times, you're 50/50 for everything. ("Maybe it'll work, maybe it won't.") And burnout syndrome — worked to death — with the opinion the chapter opened on, now defended: burnout only happens when you do something you don't enjoy. Do what you enjoy and you get lost in the process — tens of hours vanish; the kata can see, in the Agility video, that whoever made it loved making it. If you don't enjoy editing, or engineering, or creating — "there's no hope." Choose the thing you'd be lost in.

xi.

Practice —
this is your kata

Machines are beaten by preparation that looks like the machine. This week's repetitions:

Name your one hard-to-find thing — the Avengers superpower that makes you unfungible. If you can't say it in a sentence, that's the work before any application.
Rebuild your file portfolio-first: the link above the words, three best pieces, twenty-second stranger test. "The minute you make a portfolio, you make their lives easier — and you're ahead of 99% of people."
Run the prompt test on yourself: take a real task from your target job, prompt it out loud with a witness — and watch what your questions reveal about the whole CHAMPR stack.
Audit your P-letter: where are you unproven but priced like the proven — and what's the smallest proof you could ship this month to leave the goldilocks zone?
Check yourself against the syndromes honestly — victim, messiah, ostrich, Peter Pan, I-know-best, burnout — and name the one you're closest to hosting.
xii.

Sit with these

— If a hiring manager gave my file fifteen seconds tomorrow, which letter of CHAMPR survives the skim?

— Which of the six syndromes would my last manager say I was closest to — and would they be right?

— Where am I still sending words into a machine built to skip them — "Sir, I'll do anything, give me job" — instead of a thing it can judge?

— And the quit test, turned on myself honestly: at my current company, what would they feel if I walked in tomorrow and quit — panic, three months, or relief?

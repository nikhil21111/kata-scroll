i.

The deck in
the house

Three years before this session, in a house, Varun made a deck for a small core team. Its contents, shown on screen as a relic: "I want to be the best company in video — and later on in video games. 100% bootstrapped." Not a strategy document; a prophecy with a lock on the door. And the reason for the bootstrap wasn't virtue — it was honesty: the vision was so weird that nobody would have funded it anyway. Around the same time he'd published a video called Yes, AI Will Take Your Creative Job — balanced in content, incendiary in title — and been absolutely thrashed for it, because this was pre-Midjourney, when nobody believed AI could be creative. He had early access to DALL·E. He had seen it. The company that exists now was only possible because the AI tools came out — the unit economics wouldn't have made sense before.

The scoreboard as the session opens: close to 400 employees, 40,000 square feet of office, profitable from almost the first month, profitable three years running — completely bootstrapped. Whatever we've done, the kata says simply, has worked. And the other constant planted early: we believed in distribution three years ago — now everyone has woken up to it. This chapter is the play-by-play of what believing it early actually built.

ii.

The opposite
of Reddit

The founding method, stated as a confession: they did the opposite of what Reddit and Twitter were saying. And the kata names each heresy. Turn each card for what the world said, what Aeos did, and the principle underneath. The last one is the quiet one: every young engineer in Bangalore wanted to work out of HSR — so Aeos built in North Bangalore, in Hebbal, to be away from the unprofitable-startup mentality: the meta-game where people hop between funded, dying companies, extracting salary until each one collapses. Let's just get out of this mentality — go build first principles.

一
"Don't build wrappers"
the consensus, 3 years ago

A wrapper: an app built on someone else's model — GPT, Claude — without owning the foundation. The internet's verdict was clear. So Aeos built wrappers.

turn the card ↻

what happened

God in a Box on WhatsApp when the GPT API first landed — viral. AlphaCTR for thumbnails. The models commoditized anyway; the use cases were the value.

the principle

When the foundation is a commodity, fighting it is fighting the tide. Build on what's cheap and getting cheaper.

↺ return to the front

二
"Services don't scale"
if you want a big company

Everyone who wanted to build something big was told: don't do services. So Aeos built two services companies — and both became pillars.

turn the card ↻

what happened

Yaas (YouTube as a service) and Labs (tech for content) — the profitable core of the ecosystem, feeding off each other.

the principle

The moat was never the business model category. The moat is access to talent and distribution — get good talent and they'll build great products; get distribution and nothing dies quietly.

↺ return to the front

三
"Do one thing at once"
the focus gospel

True with a nuance nobody teaches: single thing versus multiple things. Aeos ran several small parallel bets — with very high failure tolerance.

turn the card ↻

the nuance

Many things, each small enough to fail without grief. If it fails, it's okay — next; we'll do the next thing. The failure tolerance is the feature, not a flaw being excused.

the result

Five entities, all profitable — the parallel bets kept the ones that worked.

↺ return to the front

四
"Raise money"
the default path

Varun had raised before. This time: nobody could be convinced — the vision was too weird, distribution's importance not yet common knowledge.

turn the card ↻

the decision

"I stopped trying to convince people. We'll do it. We'll prove it — and maybe then we'll be able to raise." Turns out they didn't need to: prove it, and what do you need money for?

the discipline it bought

Zero funding meant nobody was coming to save them — cost stayed low, overhiring never happened, and every day carried the honest knowledge: if we're wrong, we're dead.

↺ return to the front

iii.

First principles,
permanent fear

The umbrella over all the contrarian bets: first principles thinking — question every known idea about how to build a company, how to do content. People said you can't build 50 channels; three years later they'd built 50 channels. Break the problem to its individual components, solve each one, keep the tolerance to fail ("I had failed enough times in my life — who cares if one or two more things fail"), and be willing to discard ideas that don't work — including the overdone ones: everyone who wanted to build a social network was still living inside The Social Network movie, years after the world shifted. The skill is knowing when the world is shifting — and re-seeing everything from scratch. First principles is the exact opposite of status thinking: forget what other people think. (The house joke: "I just made sure my core team couldn't go to Indiranagar and HSR — that solved the problem.")

And the emotional engine underneath, confessed near the chapter's end but belonging here: permanent fear. The t-shirt says trying my best and failing often — posted on Twitter every two months. It's easy to believe you're right about everything when you're young and untested; run a few companies through their arcs of up-down-up-down and it becomes very hard to believe anything is permanent. Fear of being wrong, of automation arriving too fast, of a competitor doing it differently and knocking them out. Bootstrapped is true hard mode — the error margin is zero. In a funded company, salaries flow from a VC's promise; here, money flows only because products and services are provided, and the world is brutal: what people say they'll pay for and what they actually pay for are two different things — learned the hard way. Listen to everyone; believe only the buying behavior.

"When you're bootstrapped, you can't be wrong. Your error margin is zero."— the discipline nobody opts into
iv.

The gold rush's
scarce resource

Act one begins with an engineer's fieldwork. The average person was spending five hours a day on video — not reading blogs, watching. So Varun asked the diagnostic question: what new app have you downloaded? Nothing — category saturation: everyone already has their Spotify, their Zomato-or-Swiggy; nothing changes until a real shift arrives (like quick-commerce did). Then the second question: how many new YouTubers have you followed? — and people lit up, sharing new creators constantly. The behavioral shift was total: following creators, which ten years ago was cringe (he said it about his own wife, Achina, and admits it) was now the default. So: what company produces for this? The professionals were TV-focused news media — hate TV, because advertisers — and the amateurs were pandemic-era creators trying. First principles: what happens on TV should happen on YouTube. Advertisers will come here too. Maybe we're early. Fine — take the earlier bet.

Then the discovery that built everything: in a content gold rush, the scarce resource is the video editor. Nobody knew this until they needed one — Okus would hand over a contact, then another, then "I'm out." LinkedIn posts drew nothing: editors were paid ₹5–10k a month; it wasn't a serious career. And here the kata credits its true origin: Achina's brainchild. She would make a great channel — and the channel would have the best editing. Okus and Martin, who knew editing, cut the early videos superbly themselves; it became the brand. And the hiring pain produced the accidental pivot: one day the three of them were chatting and Achina simply said — "I think we should teach video editing." Not thought through. "It shows you how much of life is: you just get started, and then some idea comes." All the plans for what Aeos became came after that insight.

The result: the world's largest video editing school — sitting out of Hebbal. The uncool thing; for two years, no competition, because nobody thought training video editors was valuable even while consuming oceans of content (somebody has to make it). Nine or ten cohorts; 300–400 students each; 3,000+ editors trained; exceptional placements, pure supply-and-demand — everyone started a podcast, every brand wanted content, and salaries became competitive (join Aeos and ten companies ping you on LinkedIn). And the second-smartest decision, learned from watching Byju's burn: never become an edtech. Small, tight, focused on outcomes — and then build the media network instead.

"I think we should teach video editing."— Achina, casually, building the moat
v.

Own the stack

Here the kata isolates the decision that separates it from every funded alternate-history: an investor, seeing a school at 300,000 students, would have said do more of this — a 3-million-student edtech. Instead: "That doesn't make sense. We should own more along the stack." If the editor is the scarce resource in the gold rush, then build as many of your own channels as possible — own the distribution too. Out came the network: AV first, then the Varun Mayya channel (9 lakh then, over a million on Instagram now), AVTV daily, Overpowered, Breakdown (now run by Rahul Matur), Full Disclosure for careers — long form and short form in every vertical — plus the 100x investment, taught to do content.

And the mechanism that Munger — via Poor Charlie's Almanack, threaded through this whole session — promised: all the benefits of a company or an individual come at scale. At a million followers, the inbox floods: thirty to fifty emails a day, some trash, some "hello sir" and nothing — and some, the US embassy asking to collaborate. The OpenAI collab reel when GPT-4 images launched in India: millions of views, routinely repeatable now. And the full-circle moment the kata savors: in 2015, fresh out of college, Varun emailed seventy investors for his first startup — Sam Altman was one more rejection. With scale, the same door opens from the other side. "We had so many channels, we just started drowning in opportunities — and some of those we picked up and built businesses out of."

vi.

Yaas — the playbook
business

The opportunity materialized act by act. Zoho reached out — can you do a channel for us? can you experiment? — and out came Atlas for visas, Clearly Tripping for travel, Builder Central for no-code: the channels in your feed that all come from the same place. The scaling mechanism is chapter seven's thesis arriving as commerce: they wrote everything down — the editing, the writing, the thinking, the channel management — all the secrets. "Once you start writing these things down, person number two can do it, person number three can do it. And then you hire well and offer them the playbook." An ecosystem where people look next to each other and copy what worked on that video. Then the roster: Markets by Zerodha (long-form editing), NRI Shala for NRIs, Vitamin Pop for Vodafone, Fincredibles for a bank — close to forty channels now on Yaas, and the company went from 50–60 people to ~400.

The flywheel underneath: the school's best editors flowed into Yaas — the placement rate shot up, the school still artificially locked smaller for quality — and editor + good writer + channel manager became a repeatable unit. The talent pipeline and the services business feeding each other: the bento box starting to assemble itself.

vii.

Labs — the clown
video

Act four begins as an existential crisis: I'm an engineer — I studied computer science — I should be doing something more technical. And the realization that doubles as strategy: most people who do well at content are not technical, so they never ask whether technology can make content better. That gap is a moat. Technology, after all, cannot keep its hands off any field. So Harshat set up Aeos Labs as an experimental hub — fine-tune models, build a thumbnail model, put GPT on WhatsApp, have fun.

The origin story deserves its exact date. February 18, 2023: the script-to-creator experiment — an open-source model called Ki for audio, wave-to-lip for the video, a Telugu clip Varun stitched to his own moving mouth. The output was very bad. "Some of the people I showed it to laughed at me — you look like a clown making videos like this." A year later: absolutely crushing it. They had productized being a creator — something unseen in his nine, ten years of making videos. Even now it's surreal: "Sometimes I tell Achina — I can't believe this is my voice." The number-one thing everyone said at the time: nobody will watch an AI presenter. The whole room, honestly, believed it. Varun didn't believe it either — "it's going to look a little inauthentic — but we were trying anyway." Train, discard, train, discard — so many models — until the workflow emerged. The A/B test settled it: the same channel, AI and non-AI videos — no difference in views, likes, or comments.

The receipts, delivered deadpan: at Davos — first World Economic Forum invitation — YouTube's own guide to Davos listed them as the world's largest AI avatar. The power law at work: the simplest thing they'd built skyrocketed — and it only worked because they were clever enough to edit over the parts that didn't make sense, which was only possible because a video editing school sat next door. Hundreds of millions of views a month; human-written scripts + AI avatars + editors = a productized service that scales. And the strategic read underneath: models commoditize (a "greatest ever" model ships, then another, then both drop prices) — there is no moat in building the model — so Labs branched into use cases: the Indian Police Force on Amazon Prime, face-swapping with NBCS, AlphaCTR for thumbnails, the Mirzapur WhatsApp campaign (send your face; choose buddhi or bal), and Video Vault — because Frame.io lives in the cloud and podcasts are hundreds of gigabytes, so the review software was built to run on the office NAS. Labs: content plus technology — a very weird combination, with a commercial entity attached, because experimenters grow up and ask about career paths.

"Sometimes I tell Achina — I can't believe this is my voice."— the clown video, one year later
viii.

The bento box

The session's own diagram for the whole company: a bento box — sambar in one corner, rice in another, chapati in its own little section. The Aeos ecosystem, compartment by compartment: talent (AVTV's channel and the world's largest editing school; 100x Engineers as an investment — feeding Labs the engineering talent it couldn't hire, because "we had not raised a hundred million dollars — we needed to create our own talent pool"); services (Yaas; Labs); distribution (the Varun Mayya channel, AVTV, Breakdown, Overpowered, Full Disclosure, 100x, Mr. Nerf and many more); apps (God in a Box, AlphaCTR, Video Vault); and the emerging segment — Aeos Games in its proving stage, plus Headshot, the mobile gaming controller, an investment in a country that "can't all afford a PS5." The 100x bet paid its own dividends: the Meta B2B curriculum (generative AI for marketeers, LLMs for developers) was driven by them; they work closely with the OpenAI Academy, and OpenAI counts them as community partners in India.

ix.

Five profitable things
at once

The structural insight everyone misses while staring at the box: everyone else was building point solutions; Aeos wanted the entire video ecosystem — content, talent, software, infra, and ideas treated as ingredients of a bigger ecosystem: build the individual units, get really good at each, and together the outcome is better because you get more shots at goal. Learning loops through the comments — the best way people learn is from comments; you want to prove them wrong. Say yes to what's in your domain when opportunity arrives. And the result stated as a quiet boast: imagine doing five things at once and being profitable on all five.

The structure that makes it possible is the chapter's second big teaching: every entity has its own CEOs and its own co-founding teams, with Aeos as one co-founder among them. Yaas is Rohit, Lavina, Ronit and Aeos; Labs is Harshat and team and Aeos. The lone-wolf engineer is a myth — you only build big things when teams as motivated as you are helping. And the philosophy underneath that, which the kata states as a correction to a widespread misunderstanding of business: people think to make money you must screw someone else. "You need to generate and share wealth with enough people — because without incentives, you lose your best talent. You're constantly losing your best talent." The compensation and equity aren't generosity; they're the physics of keeping the box together.

"You need to generate and share wealth with enough people — without incentives, you lose your best talent."— the ecosystem's constitution
x.

The lollapalooza
method

How do you pick the next thing to believe in early? Munger again — the lollapalooza effect: when multiple things are all going in the same direction, bet on it, because the result is either massive or arrives far faster than anyone expects. For Aeos the aligned winds were: AI avatars getting better, content demand exploding, billionaires starting podcasts, and — the human factor — an inherent need to be part of the content space (the kata's own analogy for working there: backstage at WWE during the attitude era). Aspiration makes hiring easy; try staffing a garbage-collection company the same way.

Then the future thesis, offered for the audience to call him an idiot about — the games bet. The Switch 2's "10× faster" claim is mostly upscaling (DLSS has grown 1 → 2 → 3.5 → 4; 30 fps → 300 fps). Phones are getting beefier GPUs and NPUs — not for gaming, but for generative AI, which runs locally on the device (the erase-a-person feature isn't in the cloud). Unreal is optimizing ferociously. Text-to-3D keeps improving (3D artists are where photographers were three years ago — pointless fighting technology; every resume already has GPT in it). Subscriptions are how people want to buy games — but the economics don't work for $100M productions — and Clair Obscur: Expedition 33 just proved a 30-person, inexperienced-but-different team on Unreal can make game-of-the-year-candidate work ("I hate the word AAA — it's a marketing term"). Five effects, one direction. Hence the two parallel bets: Headshot — if phones keep getting powerful, why buy a Switch instead of a controller that charges while you play, with Labs building the software and launcher (Karan runs it; merciless about what doesn't work) — and PC games, not simple games — the audience that can afford games wants the Death Stranding screenshots, not 2D; those same titles retroactively run on phones four years later; and India doesn't buy games, it pirates them, unless mobile — BGMI alone does $200M+ while Raji and every Indian PC game ever made is a fraction of a fraction. The 14,000-hours-of-Dota honesty: he doesn't want to make mobile games. Build the team, the stories, and the infrastructure now — the OptiTrack mocap upgrade is a one-time investment that pays for itself across ten or twenty years of games — at the cost economics of content three years ago. They were proved wrong once; hopefully twice.

xi.

The photo album

The kata closes with the early-days photographs, and they deserve the space because they carry its two deepest lessons. The images: the first 100x meeting — Sid's strange haircut, Sudhan confused, Harshat's leg entering frame; the Museum of the Future video shot in Dubai, where they lost the footage like idiots and reshot it off their own screens in the 1,200-square-foot Prestige Westwoods house; the exercise ball for work-life balance; Embassy Grove; Okus editing at 3 am; Sedant and Tejas coding through the night in the room Sedant called the aquarium; the room that always smelled of cat until the cat ran away; the BMW video where someone played F1 in a camera mode driving exactly the way Pierre did — and for some reason, backward; the first deepfake — DeepFaceLab, Achina's body with Varun's face, hideous, live; the 100k cake; Saga's broken leg at 3 am and everyone going to the hospital to scare him further; the early AV team eating dosa off the floor.

Then the photograph that teaches the retention lesson: Achina's meeting with the early AV team — "how many can you recognize? Sumit, Dwanch. That's it. Everyone else is gone." They left for 10% raises; nobody knows if those companies are still around. The people who stayed have done very well in life. The kata's formulation, earned: "When something is going very fast, you should probably stay on board — it's very hard to find those slip streams in life." And the arc that proves the company's promise: Ronit — who runs close to 300 people today, spectacularly well, financially and otherwise — was once a kid in the Avalon Discord community, making a video about how it changed his life. The correction Varun would send back in time: "you shouldn't have built products FOR them — you should have built products WITH them." Martin, Ronit, Laksha — the community's rewards are still arriving. Anyone, anywhere in Aeos, can rise to the top — age irrelevant; can you lead from inside. The final jab at the four-options-in-mind generation: "It's too easy today to detail your opinions. Can you convert that opinion into a career? Can you take any of them seriously?" His only remaining job: find the next thirty or forty who can — in a market with, as far as he can see, no limit to the size of the content business in India.

"When something is going very fast, you should probably stay on board."— the slip-stream rule
xii.

Practice —
this is your kata

Origins are instructions in disguise. This month's repetitions:

Write your house deck: the weird vision nobody would fund, one page, honest — and mark which parts you believe enough to bootstrap.
List the three loudest pieces of advice in your field's Reddit — then first-principles one of them: is it still true, or is it The Social Network of your industry?
Find your gold rush's scarce resource — not the gold, the shovels: what does everyone in your boom consume and nobody trains, school, or supply?
Run one lollapalooza audit: list every force moving in the same direction in your field — if you find four or five, that's your bet.
Make one Feb-18-2023 of your own: ship the clown version of the thing you believe in — and let the laughter keep a date with next year.
xiii.

Sit with these

— What is my deck-in-the-house — the vision too weird to convince anyone of — and am I building it, or waiting for permission to?

— Which consensus am I living inside that I've never actually tested against first principles?

— If my error margin were zero — nobody coming to save me — what would I stop spending on tomorrow morning?

— And the photo-album question: in the fast thing I'm inside — am I staying on board, or negotiating a 10% exit from a slip stream?
